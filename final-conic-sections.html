Part 1 — What a Conic Is Unit 4

A Plane Slicing a Double Cone from slides

A conic section is the curve you get when a plane cuts a right circular cone. The angle and position of the plane decide which curve appears:

Plane is…CurveReal-world example (from slides)
Perpendicular to the cone's axisCircleWheels and gears
Tilted, but cuts only one nappe, closed curveEllipsePlanetary orbits
Parallel to a side (generator) of the coneParabolaSatellite dishes, car headlights
Steep enough to cut both nappesHyperbolaCooling towers, navigation systems

Why parabolas show up in dishes and headlights: every ray arriving parallel to the axis reflects through the focus (and a bulb placed at the focus sends light out parallel). That reflection property is exactly what the lecture's dish and headlight pictures illustrate.

Part 2 — Focus, Directrix & Eccentricity Unit 4

One Definition for All Three from slides

A conic is the locus of a point \(P\) whose distance to a fixed point (the focus \(F\)) divided by its distance to a fixed line (the directrix) is a constant \(e\):

$$ e = \frac{PF}{PD} = \frac{\text{distance to focus}}{\text{distance to directrix}} $$
EccentricityConicShape intuition
\(e = 0\)CirclePerfectly round
\(0 < e < 1\)EllipseCloser to 0 = rounder, closer to 1 = more stretched
\(e = 1\)Parabola\(PF = PD\) exactly
\(e > 1\)HyperbolaLarger \(e\) = wider-opening branches
Eccentricity measures how far a conic is from being a circle. Use it as a sanity check: an ellipse answer above 1, or a hyperbola answer below 1, is wrong.
The vocabulary
  • Vertex — where the curve turns; the point on the curve closest to the focus and to the directrix.
  • Focus — the fixed point; tells you which way the curve "points", and drives the reflection property.
  • Directrix — the fixed line.
  • Axis of symmetry — the line through the focus perpendicular to the directrix.
  • Latus rectum — the chord through a focus, perpendicular to the axis.

Part 3 — The Parabola Unit 4

Definition & Standard Form from slides

A parabola is the locus of a point that stays equally far from the focus and from the directrix: \(PF = PD\), so \(e = 1\).

$$ y^2 = 4ax \qquad (a > 0) $$
For \(y^2 = 4ax\)Value
Vertex\((0, 0)\)
Focus\((a, 0)\)
Directrix\(x = -a\)
Axis of symmetry\(y = 0\) (the \(x\)-axis)
Length of latus rectum\(4a\)
Eccentricity\(e = 1\)

The Four Orientations

Which variable is squared tells you the axis; the sign tells you the direction. In every case \(a > 0\) and the vertex is at the origin.

EquationOpensFocusDirectrixAxis
\(y^2 = 4ax\)Right\((a, 0)\)\(x = -a\)\(y = 0\)
\(y^2 = -4ax\)Left\((-a, 0)\)\(x = a\)\(y = 0\)
\(x^2 = 4ay\)Up\((0, a)\)\(y = -a\)\(x = 0\)
\(x^2 = -4ay\)Down\((0, -a)\)\(y = a\)\(x = 0\)
The squared variable is the one that stays symmetric. \(y^2\) → the curve is symmetric in \(y\), so its axis is horizontal and it opens left or right.
Quick example

\(y^2 = 8x\): here \(4a = 8\), so \(a = 2\).

Vertex \((0,0)\), focus \((2,0)\), directrix \(x = -2\), latus rectum length \(8\), opens right.

⚠ Always find \(a\) by matching the number to \(4a\) — not to \(a\). In \(y^2 = 8x\), \(a = 2\), not 8.

Part 4 — The Ellipse Unit 4

Two Equivalent Definitions from slides

  1. Focus–directrix: the locus of \(P\) with \(PF/PD = e\), where \(e < 1\).
  2. Two foci: the locus of \(P\) whose sum of distances to two fixed points is constant: \(PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a\).
$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \qquad (a > b > 0) $$
For \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\), \(a > b\)Value
Centre\((0, 0)\)
Vertices (ends of major axis)\((\pm a, 0)\)
Co-vertices (ends of minor axis)\((0, \pm b)\)
Length of major / minor axis\(2a\) / \(2b\)
Focal distance \(c\)\(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\)
Foci\((\pm c, 0) = (\pm ae, 0)\)
Eccentricity\(e = \dfrac{c}{a} = \sqrt{1 - \dfrac{b^2}{a^2}}\)
Directrices\(x = \pm\dfrac{a}{e}\)
Length of each latus rectum\(\dfrac{2b^2}{a}\)

Horizontal or Vertical? The Larger Denominator Decides

In an ellipse, \(a^2\) is always the larger denominator. Whichever variable it sits under is the direction of the major axis.

Horizontal major axisVertical major axis
Equation\(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)\(\dfrac{x^2}{b^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{a^2} = 1\)
Vertices\((\pm a, 0)\)\((0, \pm a)\)
Co-vertices\((0, \pm b)\)\((\pm b, 0)\)
Foci\((\pm c, 0)\)\((0, \pm c)\)
Directrices\(x = \pm a/e\)\(y = \pm a/e\)
Quick example

\(\dfrac{x^2}{9} + \dfrac{y^2}{25} = 1\): the larger denominator 25 is under \(y\), so the major axis is vertical.

\(a = 5\), \(b = 3\), \(c = \sqrt{25 - 9} = 4\). Vertices \((0, \pm 5)\), foci \((0, \pm 4)\), \(e = \tfrac{4}{5}\).

⚠ Foci always lie on the major axis, inside the ellipse (\(c < a\)). If your foci come out outside the vertices, you used \(a^2 + b^2\) instead of \(a^2 - b^2\).

Part 5 — The Hyperbola Unit 4

Definition & Standard Form from slides

  1. Focus–directrix: the locus of \(P\) with \(PF/PD = e\), where \(e > 1\).
  2. Two foci: the locus of \(P\) whose difference of distances to two fixed points is constant: \(|PF_1 - PF_2| = 2a\). (This is the navigation-station picture in the slides.)
$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 $$
For \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)Value
Centre\((0, 0)\)
Vertices\((\pm a, 0)\)
Length of transverse / conjugate axis\(2a\) / \(2b\)
Focal distance \(c\)\(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\)
Foci\((\pm c, 0) = (\pm ae, 0)\)
Eccentricity\(e = \dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}{a} = \sqrt{1 + \dfrac{b^2}{a^2}}\)
Directrices\(x = \pm\dfrac{a}{e}\)
Asymptotes\(y = \pm\dfrac{b}{a}\,x\)
Length of each latus rectum\(\dfrac{2b^2}{a}\)
⚠ The slide writes the asymptotes as "\(y = \pm\tfrac{b}{a}\)" — the \(x\) is missing. The asymptotes are the lines \(y = \pm\tfrac{b}{a}x\).
Asymptote shortcut that works for every orientation and every shifted centre: replace the 1 on the right with 0 and solve for \(y\).

Horizontal or Vertical? The Positive Term Decides

Unlike the ellipse, the hyperbola does not care which denominator is bigger — \(b\) can be larger than \(a\). What matters is which squared term is positive: that variable's axis is where the branches open, and its denominator is \(a^2\).

Opens left–rightOpens up–down
Equation\(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\)\(\dfrac{y^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{x^2}{b^2} = 1\)
Vertices\((\pm a, 0)\)\((0, \pm a)\)
Foci\((\pm c, 0)\)\((0, \pm c)\)
Asymptotes\(y = \pm\dfrac{b}{a}x\)\(y = \pm\dfrac{a}{b}x\)
Quick example

\(\dfrac{x^2}{9} - \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1\): \(a = 3\), \(b = 4\) (here \(b > a\), and that's fine).

\(c = \sqrt{9+16} = 5\). Vertices \((\pm 3, 0)\), foci \((\pm 5, 0)\), \(e = \tfrac{5}{3}\), asymptotes \(y = \pm\tfrac{4}{3}x\).

Part 6 — Ellipse vs Hyperbola, Side by Side

The Differences That Get Mixed Up

EllipseHyperbola
Sign between the terms\(+\)\(-\)
Which denominator is \(a^2\)?The larger oneThe positive term's
Relation for \(c\)\(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\)\(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\)

Part 7 — Translated Conics Textbook · Worksheet Q1

Shifting the Centre to \((h, k)\) textbook

Replace \(x\) with \(x - h\) and \(y\) with \(y - k\). The shape is unchanged; every point just moves by \((h, k)\).

ConicShifted equationKey points
Parabola (opens right)\((y-k)^2 = 4a(x-h)\)Vertex \((h,k)\), focus \((h+a, k)\), directrix \(x = h - a\)
Parabola (opens up)\((x-h)^2 = 4a(y-k)\)Vertex \((h,k)\), focus \((h, k+a)\), directrix \(y = k - a\)
Ellipse\(\dfrac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1\)Centre \((h,k)\), vertices \((h \pm a, k)\), foci \((h \pm c, k)\)
Hyperbola\(\dfrac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1\)Centre \((h,k)\), foci \((h \pm c, k)\), asymptotes \(y - k = \pm\tfrac{b}{a}(x-h)\)
⚠ Watch the signs: \((x - 3)^2\) means \(h = +3\), and \((y + 2)^2\) means \(k = -2\). The centre is \((3, -2)\).

Identifying a Conic from \(Ax^2 + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\)

With no \(xy\) term, just compare the coefficients of \(x^2\) and \(y^2\):

CoefficientsConic
\(A = C\)Circle
\(A\) and \(C\) have the same signEllipse
\(A\) and \(C\) have opposite signsHyperbola
One of \(A, C\) is zeroParabola

(Degenerate cases — a point, a pair of lines, or no graph — are possible, but you'll spot those when the right-hand side comes out zero or negative after completing the square.)

Completing the square — the method
  1. Group the \(x\) terms and the \(y\) terms; move the constant to the right
  2. Factor out the coefficient of \(x^2\) and of \(y^2\) from their groups
  3. Complete each square: inside the bracket, add \((\text{half the coefficient of } x)^2\) — e.g. \(x^2 - 4x\) needs \(+4\)
  4. Add coefficient × that amount to the right-hand side to keep the equation balanced
  5. Divide through so the right-hand side is 1, then read off \(h, k, a, b\)
Worked example

Identify and analyse \(4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0\).

\(A = 4\), \(C = 9\), same sign → ellipse.

$$ 4(x^2 - 4x) + 9(y^2 + 2y) = 11 $$
$$ 4(x^2 - 4x + 4) + 9(y^2 + 2y + 1) $$
$$ = 11 + 4(4) + 9(1) $$
$$ 4(x-2)^2 + 9(y+1)^2 = 36 $$
$$ \frac{(x-2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y+1)^2}{4} = 1 $$

Centre \((2, -1)\), \(a = 3\), \(b = 2\), \(c = \sqrt{9-4} = \sqrt5\).

Vertices \((2 \pm 3, -1) = (5, -1), (-1, -1)\); foci \((2 \pm \sqrt5, -1)\); \(e = \tfrac{\sqrt5}{3} \approx 0.745\).

⚠ The single most common error: adding 4 and 1 to the right-hand side instead of \(4 \times 4\) and \(9 \times 1\). What you add inside a bracket gets multiplied by the factor outside it.

Part 8 — Rotation of Axes Textbook · Worksheet Q4

When There's an \(xy\) Term textbook

The general second-degree equation is

$$ Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0 $$

A non-zero \(B\) means the conic is tilted. Rotating the axes by the right angle \(\theta\) removes the \(xy\) term.

Step 1 — Identify it with the discriminant
\(B^2 - 4AC\)Conic
\(< 0\)Ellipse (or circle)
\(= 0\)Parabola
\(> 0\)Hyperbola
The discriminant doesn't change under rotation — so you can classify the conic before doing any rotating at all.
Step 2 — Find the angle
$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B}, \qquad 0^\circ < \theta < 90^\circ $$

If \(A = C\), then \(\cot 2\theta = 0\), so \(2\theta = 90^\circ\) and \(\theta = 45^\circ\).

Otherwise, get \(\cos 2\theta\) from \(\cot 2\theta\) (draw the triangle), then use the half-angle formulas:

$$ \cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}} $$
$$ \sin\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}} $$
Step 3 — Substitute the rotation formulas
$$ x = x'\cos\theta - y'\sin\theta $$
$$ y = x'\sin\theta + y'\cos\theta $$

Or skip the expanding and use the coefficient formulas directly:

New coefficientFormula
\(A'\)\(A\cos^2\theta + B\cos\theta\sin\theta + C\sin^2\theta\)
\(B'\)\(0\) (that's the point)
\(C'\)\(A\sin^2\theta - B\sin\theta\cos\theta + C\cos^2\theta\)
\(D'\)\(D\cos\theta + E\sin\theta\)
\(E'\)\(-D\sin\theta + E\cos\theta\)
\(F'\)\(F\) (unchanged)
Check your work: \(A' + C' = A + C\) always. If it doesn't, something went wrong.
Step 4 — Complete the square in \(x', y'\) as in Part 7
Worked example — \(xy = 1\)

Rewrite as \(xy - 1 = 0\): \(A = 0\), \(B = 1\), \(C = 0\), \(F = -1\).

\(B^2 - 4AC = 1 > 0\) → hyperbola. \(A = C\) → \(\theta = 45^\circ\), so \(\cos\theta = \sin\theta = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\).

$$ A' = 0 + 1\cdot\tfrac12 + 0 = \tfrac12 $$
$$ C' = 0 - 1\cdot\tfrac12 + 0 = -\tfrac12 $$
$$ \tfrac12 x'^2 - \tfrac12 y'^2 - 1 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{x'^2}{2} - \frac{y'^2}{2} = 1 $$

A hyperbola with \(a = b = \sqrt2\), rotated 45°. Check: \(A' + C' = 0 = A + C\). ✓

Finding \(\sin\theta\), \(\cos\theta\) when the angle isn't 45°

Suppose \(\cot 2\theta = \tfrac{7}{24}\). Then a right triangle with adjacent 7, opposite 24 has hypotenuse 25, so \(\cos 2\theta = \tfrac{7}{25}\).

$$ \cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 7/25}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{16}{25}} = \frac45 $$
$$ \sin\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 - 7/25}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{25}} = \frac35 $$

Part 9 — Exam Strategy & Common Mistakes

Reading the question — what to reach for

You're given…First move
Only one squared variableParabola — match to \(4a\), read off vertex / focus / directrix
Two squared terms, same signEllipse — larger denominator is \(a^2\), \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\)
Two squared terms, opposite signsHyperbola — positive term is \(a^2\), \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\)
Linear terms \(Dx\), \(Ey\) presentComplete the square first
An \(xy\) termDiscriminant to classify, then rotate by \(\cot 2\theta = \tfrac{A-C}{B}\)
"Find the eccentricity"\(e = c/a\) — sanity-check the range

The mistakes that actually cost marks

  1. Wrong \(c\) formula — ellipse subtracts, hyperbola adds.
  2. Picking \(a\) by size in a hyperbola — it's the positive term, not the bigger number.
  3. Forgetting to shift — after completing the square, vertices and foci are \((h \pm a, k)\), not \((\pm a, 0)\).
  4. Sign of \(h\) and \(k\) — \((x + 2)^2\) means \(h = -2\).
  5. Not multiplying what you add — when completing the square with a factor outside the bracket.
  6. Parabola \(a\) from \(4a\) — divide the coefficient by 4.
  7. Axis lengths vs semi-axes — the major axis is \(2a\), not \(a\).
  8. Asymptotes of a shifted hyperbola — they pass through \((h, k)\), not the origin.

Ten-second checks

  • Substitute a vertex back into the original equation — it should give 0.
  • Ellipse: \(e < 1\) and foci between the vertices. Hyperbola: \(e > 1\) and foci beyond the vertices.
  • Parabola: the vertex is exactly halfway between the focus and the directrix.
  • Rotation: \(A' + C' = A + C\), and \(B'\) should come out 0.
  • After completing the square, the right-hand side must be positive for a real ellipse or hyperbola.
