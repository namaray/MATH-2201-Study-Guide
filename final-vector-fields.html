Scalar field — temperature in a room from slides

\(T(x,y,z) = 20 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2\). At \((1,2,1)\): \(T = 20 + 1 + 4 + 1 = 26^\circ\text{C}\).

The temperature varies from point to point, but at each point it has exactly one value — that's what makes it a scalar field.

Vector field — evaluate at a point from slides

\(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = \langle x^2,\ yz,\ zx\rangle\) at \(P(2,1,3)\):

$$ \mathbf{F}(2,1,3) = \langle 4,\ 3,\ 6\rangle = 4\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j} + 6\mathbf{k} $$

The magnitude \(|\mathbf{F}| = \sqrt{16 + 9 + 36} = \sqrt{61}\) is the "strength" of the field there.

Contours (level curves) from slides

Fixing \(f(x,y) = c\) gives a contour: a curve joining points where the function has the same value — like the height lines on a hiking map. In 3D, \(F(x,y,z) = c\) is a level surface.

Part 2 — The Gradient Unit 6

Scalar Field In, Vector Field Out from slides

Stand on a hill with height \(z = f(x,y)\). Of all the directions you could walk, the gradient points the way the height increases fastest.

$$ \nabla = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\mathbf{k} $$
$$ (\text{the "del" operator}) $$
$$ \nabla f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\mathbf{k} = \langle f_x,\ f_y,\ f_z\rangle $$
What the gradient tells you
  • Direction of \(\nabla f\): steepest increase of \(f\).
  • Magnitude \(|\nabla f|\): that steepest rate of increase.
  • \(-\nabla f\): steepest decrease (the way water flows downhill).
  • \(\nabla f\) is always perpendicular to the contour through the point.
Example 1 — metal plate from slides

\(T(x,y) = 100 + 4x + 3y\) (°C, metres). \(T_x = 4\), \(T_y = 3\), so \(\nabla T = 4\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j}\) everywhere.

Temperature rises fastest in the direction \(4\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j}\), at \(|\nabla T| = 5\) °C per metre.

Example 2 — evaluate at a point from slides

\(f(x,y) = x^2 + 3xy + y^2\): \(f_x = 2x + 3y\), \(f_y = 3x + 2y\).

$$ \nabla f = (2x + 3y)\mathbf{i} + (3x + 2y)\mathbf{j} $$
$$ \nabla f(1,2) = \langle 8,\ 7\rangle $$
⚠ Find the general \(\nabla f\) first, then substitute the point. Substituting before differentiating gives zero.

Part 3 — Directional Derivatives Unit 6

Rate of Change in a Chosen Direction from slides

The gradient answers "which way is fastest?". The directional derivative answers "if I walk this way, how fast does \(f\) change?" — it generalises \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) to any direction.

$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} $$
$$ \mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|} \;(\text{a unit vector}) $$
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = f_x\,a + f_y\,b \quad\text{for } \mathbf{u} = \langle a, b\rangle $$
The method
  1. Compute \(\nabla f\) and evaluate it at the point
  2. Turn the direction into a unit vector: \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}/|\mathbf{v}|\). If you're given two points \(P \to Q\), use \(\mathbf{v} = \overrightarrow{PQ}\)
  3. Dot product: \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}\)
⚠ Normalise the direction vector. Using \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 3,4\rangle\) instead of \(\langle \tfrac35, \tfrac45\rangle\) makes your answer 5 times too big. This is the most common mistake in the unit.
Since \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = |\nabla f|\cos\theta\):
Direction\(D_{\mathbf{u}}f\)
Along \(\nabla f\) (\(\theta = 0\))Maximum: \(|\nabla f|\)
Opposite \(\nabla f\) (\(\theta = \pi\))Minimum: \(-|\nabla f|\)
Perpendicular to \(\nabla f\) (along the contour)\(0\)
Example 1 from slides

\(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\) at \((1,2)\), toward \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 3, 4\rangle\).

$$ \nabla f(1,2) = \langle 2x, 2y\rangle = \langle 2, 4\rangle $$
$$ \mathbf{u} = \tfrac15\langle 3, 4\rangle $$
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \frac{2(3) + 4(4)}{5} = \frac{22}{5} = 4.4 $$
Example 2 from slides

\(f(x,y) = x^2 + 3xy\), find \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f(1,2)\) toward \(\mathbf{v} = \langle 3, 4\rangle\).

$$ \nabla f = \langle 2x + 3y,\ 3x\rangle $$
$$ \nabla f(1,2) = \langle 8, 3\rangle $$
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \frac{8(3) + 3(4)}{5} = \frac{36}{5} = 7.2 $$

Part 4 — Normal Vectors & Tangent Planes Unit 6

The Gradient Is a Normal from slides

A normal vector is perpendicular to a surface at a point — it shows which way the surface faces.

  • Plane \(ax + by + cz = d\): normal \(\langle a, b, c\rangle\) everywhere. E.g. \(2x + 3y + 4z = 12\) → \(\mathbf{n} = \langle 2, 3, 4\rangle\).
  • Curved surface \(F(x,y,z) = C\): the normal changes from point to point, and equals \(\nabla F\) evaluated there.
$$ \mathbf{n} = \nabla F(x_0, y_0, z_0) $$
$$ = \left\langle \frac{\partial F}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y},\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial z} \right\rangle_{(x_0,y_0,z_0)} $$
Example — sphere from slides

Normal to \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 25\) at \((3, 4, 0)\): \(\nabla F = \langle 2x, 2y, 2z\rangle = \langle 6, 8, 0\rangle\), so \(\mathbf{n} = 6\mathbf{i} + 8\mathbf{j}\) — pointing straight out from the centre, as you'd expect.

Tangent planes

The tangent plane just touches the surface at a point and is its best flat approximation there.

Surface given as…Tangent plane at \((x_0, y_0, z_0)\)
\(z = f(x,y)\) from slides\(z - z_0 = f_x(x - x_0) + f_y(y - y_0)\)
\(F(x,y,z) = C\)\(F_x(x - x_0) + F_y(y - y_0) + F_z(z - z_0) = 0\)

The second form is just "plane through a point with normal \(\nabla F\)" — the same plane equation from the midterm.

Example — tangent plane to a sphere from slides

\(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 25\) at \((0, 3, 4)\). Normal: \(\nabla F(0,3,4) = \langle 0, 6, 8\rangle\).

$$ 0(x - 0) + 6(y - 3) + 8(z - 4) = 0 $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\quad 3y + 4z = 25 $$

Check: \((0,3,4)\) gives \(9 + 16 = 25\). ✓

Part 5 — Lagrange Multipliers Unit 6

Max / Min With a Constraint from slides

  • Objective \(f(x,y)\): what you maximise or minimise.
  • Constraint \(g(x,y) = k\): the condition the variables must obey.
  • Multiplier \(\lambda\): a new unknown that links them.

At the optimum, the contour of \(f\) just touches the constraint curve, so their gradients are parallel:

$$ \nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g $$
$$ g(x,y) = k $$
  1. Write \(f_x = \lambda g_x\) and \(f_y = \lambda g_y\)
  2. Eliminate \(\lambda\) (often by dividing or equating)
  3. Substitute into the constraint and solve; evaluate \(f\) at each candidate point
Example — smallest sum of squares from slides

Minimise \(f = x^2 + y^2\) subject to \(x + y = 4\).

$$ \nabla f = \langle 2x, 2y\rangle, \quad \nabla g = \langle 1, 1\rangle $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; 2x = \lambda,\ 2y = \lambda $$

So \(x = y\); the constraint gives \(x = y = 2\), and the minimum is \(f(2,2) = 8\).

Same answer as substituting \(y = 4 - x\) and setting \(f'(x) = 4x - 8 = 0\) — the slides show both methods.

Part 6 — Divergence & Curl Unit 6

Three Operators, Three Questions from slides

OperationActs on → givesThink of
\(\nabla f\)  gradientscalar → vectorWhich way is uphill?
\(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\)  divergencevector → scalarIs stuff spreading out or gathering?
\(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\)  curlvector → vectorIs stuff rotating?

Divergence from slides

For \(\mathbf{F} = F_x\mathbf{i} + F_y\mathbf{j} + F_z\mathbf{k}\) (or \(P, Q, R\)):

$$ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z} $$

A dot product with \(\nabla\), so the result is a scalar.

\(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} > 0\)Source — the field spreads out (fluid expanding)
\(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} < 0\)Sink — the field gathers in (fluid compressing)
\(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 0\)No net expansion — incompressible flow
⚠ Each component is differentiated with respect to its own variable only: \(P\) by \(x\), \(Q\) by \(y\), \(R\) by \(z\). Not all three by everything.

Curl from slides

$$ \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ P & Q & R \end{vmatrix} $$
$$ = \left(\frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z}\right)\mathbf{i} + \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x}\right)\mathbf{j} $$
$$ + \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)\mathbf{k} $$

A cross product with \(\nabla\), so the result is a vector. If \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\) the field is irrotational.

Expand it exactly like the midterm cross product — same \(\mathbf{j}\)-term minus sign trap. Writing the middle term as \(\left(P_z - R_x\right)\) already absorbs that minus.
Two identities that always hold from slides
$$ \nabla \times (\nabla f) = \mathbf{0} $$
$$ \text{(the curl of a gradient is zero)} $$
$$ \nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) = 0 $$
$$ \text{(the divergence of a curl is zero)} $$

The first one is why "curl = 0" is the test for a conservative field in Part 7.

Worked Examples from slides

Question 1 — divergence

\(\mathbf{F} = x^2\mathbf{i} + 3xy\mathbf{j} + 2z^2\mathbf{k}\)

$$ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial (x^2)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial (3xy)}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial (2z^2)}{\partial z} $$
$$ = 2x + 3x + 4z = 5x + 4z $$
Question 2 — curl

\(\mathbf{F} = yz\,\mathbf{i} + xz\,\mathbf{j} + xy\,\mathbf{k}\), so \(P = yz\), \(Q = xz\), \(R = xy\).

$$ \nabla \times \mathbf{F} $$
$$ = (x - x)\mathbf{i} + (y - y)\mathbf{j} + (z - z)\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{0} $$

Irrotational — and indeed \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla(xyz)\), a gradient.

Question 3 — is the fluid compressible?

\(\mathbf{V} = 2x\,\mathbf{i} + 3y\,\mathbf{j} + 4z\,\mathbf{k}\)

$$ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{V} = 2 + 3 + 4 = 9 $$
  1. The divergence is \(9\).
  2. \(9 > 0\) everywhere, so the fluid is expanding (every point acts as a source).
  3. \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{V} \ne 0\), so the flow is not incompressible — it's compressible.

Part 7 — Conservative Fields & Potential Functions Unit 6

The Big Equivalence from slides

\(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative if it's the gradient of some scalar potential function \(\phi\):

$$ \mathbf{F} = \nabla\phi $$
$$ \Longleftrightarrow\quad P = \phi_x,\quad Q = \phi_y,\quad R = \phi_z $$
Conservative field ⟺ has a potential function ⟹ path independence

For a conservative field (on a simply connected region), all of these hold together:

  • \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla\phi\) for some \(\phi\)
  • \(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) depends only on the endpoints, not the path
  • \(\displaystyle\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0\) around every closed curve
Example — a gradient field from slides

\(\phi = x^2 + y^2\) gives \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla\phi = 2x\,\mathbf{i} + 2y\,\mathbf{j}\) — every vector points radially outward from the origin.

Testing Whether a Field Is Conservative

FieldConservative if…
2D: \(P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j}\) from slides\(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\)
3D: \(P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}\)\(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\):  \(R_y = Q_z,\ \ P_z = R_x,\ \ Q_x = P_y\)

The 3D test follows from the slide identity \(\nabla \times (\nabla\phi) = \mathbf{0}\): a gradient can't have curl, so a field with curl can't be a gradient. The 2D test is just the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the same thing.

⚠ The test pairs cross derivatives: \(P\) by \(y\) against \(Q\) by \(x\). Comparing \(P_x\) with \(Q_y\) is a common slip and tells you nothing.

Finding the Potential Function \(\phi\)

  1. Integrate \(P\) with respect to \(x\): \(\phi = \int P\,dx + g(y, z)\). The "constant" can depend on the other variables
  2. Differentiate that \(\phi\) with respect to \(y\) and set it equal to \(Q\) — solve for \(g_y\)
  3. Integrate to get \(g\); in 3D repeat with \(z\) and \(R\)
  4. Write \(\phi\) with a final \(+\,C\), and check \(\nabla\phi = \mathbf{F}\)
Worked example from slides

\(\mathbf{F} = (2x + 3y)\mathbf{i} + (3x + 4y)\mathbf{j}\). Test: \(P_y = 3 = Q_x\) ✓ conservative.

$$ \phi = \int (2x + 3y)\,dx = x^2 + 3xy + g(y) $$
$$ \phi_y = 3x + g'(y) = 3x + 4y $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g'(y) = 4y $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g = 2y^2 $$
$$ \phi = x^2 + 3xy + 2y^2 + C $$
⚠ Don't just integrate \(P\) by \(x\) and \(Q\) by \(y\) and add — the shared terms (like \(3xy\)) get counted twice. Always go through \(g(y)\).
Fundamental theorem for line integrals from slides
$$ \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(B) - \phi(A) $$
$$ (\text{if } \mathbf{F} = \nabla\phi) $$
If the field is conservative, skip parametrising entirely — find \(\phi\) and subtract its values at the endpoints.

Part 8 — Line Integrals Unit 6

Integrating Along a Curve from slides

An ordinary integral accumulates \(f(x)\) along the \(x\)-axis. A line integral accumulates a quantity along a curve \(C\), described by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = x(t)\mathbf{i} + y(t)\mathbf{j}\), \(a \le t \le b\). There are two kinds:

Scalar fieldVector field
Notation\(\displaystyle\int_C f\,ds\)\(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\)
UsesArc length element \(ds\)Displacement \(d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt\)
MeaningMass of a wire, area of a "curtain"Work done by a force along a path

Line Integral of a Scalar Field from slides

Parametric curve \(x = x(t),\ y = y(t)\)
$$ ds = \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt $$
$$ \int_C f(x,y)\,ds $$
$$ = \int_a^b f\big(x(t), y(t)\big)\sqrt{x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2}\;dt $$
Explicit curve \(y = g(x),\ a \le x \le b\)
$$ \int_C f(x,y)\,ds $$
$$ = \int_a^b f\big(x, g(x)\big)\sqrt{1 + [g'(x)]^2}\;dx $$
\(ds\) is the same arc-length element as Unit 2 — with \(f = 1\), \(\int_C ds\) is just the length of the curve.
Worked example from slides

\(\int_C (x + y)\,ds\), \(C\) the segment from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\): \(x = t\), \(y = t\), so \(ds = \sqrt{1 + 1}\,dt = \sqrt2\,dt\).

$$ \int_0^1 (2t)\sqrt2\,dt = 2\sqrt2\left[\frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^1 = \sqrt2 $$

Line Integral of a Vector Field from slides

Two equivalent forms
$$ \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}\big(\mathbf{r}(t)\big)\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt $$
$$ \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy \;(+\,R\,dz) $$
The method
  1. Check first: is \(\mathbf{F}\) conservative? If so, use \(\phi(B) - \phi(A)\) and stop
  2. Parametrise \(C\). A straight line from \(A\) to \(B\): \(\mathbf{r}(t) = A + t(B - A)\), \(0 \le t \le 1\)
  3. Substitute \(x(t), y(t), z(t)\) into \(\mathbf{F}\) and compute \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\)
  4. Dot them, then integrate from \(t = a\) to \(t = b\)
Example 1 — along a parabola from slides

\(\mathbf{F} = y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\), \(C: y = x^2,\ 0 \le x \le 1\). Here \(P = y\), \(Q = x\), and \(dy = 2x\,dx\).

$$ \int_C y\,dx + x\,dy $$
$$ = \int_0^1 \left[x^2 + x(2x)\right]dx $$
$$ = \int_0^1 3x^2\,dx = 1 $$
Example 2 — parametric form from slides

\(\mathbf{F} = y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\), \(x = 2t,\ y = t,\ 0 \le t \le 1\). So \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = t\,\mathbf{i} + 2t\,\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = 2\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j}\).

$$ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{r}' = 2t + 2t = 4t $$
$$ \int_0^1 4t\,dt = 2 $$
Both examples use \(\mathbf{F} = y\mathbf{i} + x\mathbf{j} = \nabla(xy)\) — conservative. Shortcut: \(\phi = xy\), so Example 1 is \(\phi(1,1) - \phi(0,0) = 1\) and Example 2 is \(\phi(2,1) - \phi(0,0) = 2\). Same answers, no integration.

Part 9 — Green's Theorem Textbook · CT4 Q15–16

Turning a Loop Integral into an Area Integral textbook

For a closed curve \(C\) traversed counterclockwise (positive orientation) around a region \(R\):

$$ \oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_R \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA $$
  1. Identify \(P\) (the \(dx\) coefficient) and \(Q\) (the \(dy\) coefficient)
  2. Compute \(Q_x - P_y\) — often a constant or a simple expression
  3. Set up the double integral over the enclosed region and evaluate (Unit 3 skills)
If \(Q_x - P_y\) is a constant \(k\), the answer is just \(k \times\) (area of \(R\)).
⚠ Order matters: it's \(Q_x - P_y\), not \(P_y - Q_x\). And the curve must be counterclockwise — clockwise flips the sign.

Notice the link to Part 7: if \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative then \(Q_x = P_y\), the integrand is zero, and \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0\) — exactly the closed-loop property.

Quick example

\(\oint_C x\,dy\) around the unit circle: \(P = 0\), \(Q = x\), so \(Q_x - P_y = 1\).

The integral equals the area enclosed: \(\pi\). (Green's theorem gives area formulas for free.)

Part 10 — Exam Strategy & Common Mistakes

Reading the question — what to reach for

The question asks…Reach for
"value of the field at a point"Substitute — no calculus
"direction of greatest increase" / "gradient"\(\nabla f\)
"maximum rate of change"\(|\nabla f|\)
"rate of change in the direction of…" / "from P toward Q"\(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u}\), unit \(\mathbf{u}\)
"normal to the surface" / "tangent plane"\(\nabla F\) at the point
"max / min subject to…"Lagrange: \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\)
"divergence" / "compressible?"\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\)
"curl" / "irrotational?"\(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\)
"is it conservative?" / "potential function"Cross-derivative test, then integrate
"work done" / \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\)Conservative? → \(\phi(B)-\phi(A)\). Otherwise parametrise
\(\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy\) around a closed boundaryGreen's theorem

The mistakes that actually cost marks

  1. Not normalising the direction in a directional derivative.
  2. Substituting the point before differentiating.
  3. Mixing up div and curl outputs — divergence is a number, curl is a vector.
  4. Wrong pairing in the conservative test — compare \(P_y\) with \(Q_x\).
  5. Double-counting terms in \(\phi\) — always go through \(g(y)\).
  6. Forgetting \(+C\) when asked for a potential function "including the arbitrary constant".
  7. Forgetting \(ds\) or \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\) — substituting the curve into \(f\) or \(\mathbf{F}\) is only half the job.
  8. Green's sign and orientation — \(Q_x - P_y\), counterclockwise.

Ten-second checks

  • Found \(\phi\)? Differentiate it — you should get back \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\) exactly.
  • A directional derivative can never exceed \(|\nabla f|\) in size.
  • \(\nabla f\) at a point should be perpendicular to the contour there.
  • For a conservative field, a straight-line parametrisation and \(\phi(B) - \phi(A)\) must agree.
  • Green's with constant integrand: answer = constant × area of the region.
