Part 1 — Vectors in 3-Space Unit 1

The Basics

A vector \(\mathbf{a} = a_1\mathbf{i} + a_2\mathbf{j} + a_3\mathbf{k} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3\rangle\) has both magnitude and direction.

$$ |\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{a}} = \frac{\mathbf{a}}{|\mathbf{a}|} $$

The vector from point \(A\) to point \(B\) is always head minus tail:

$$ \overrightarrow{AB} = B - A = \langle b_1-a_1,\; b_2-a_2,\; b_3-a_3\rangle $$
⚠ \(\overrightarrow{AB} = B - A\), not \(A - B\). Getting this backwards flips every sign downstream.

Dot Product — "how much do these align?"

Produces a scalar.

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3 = |\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta $$
$$ \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|} $$
Perpendicular \(\iff \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 0\)
Worked example — angle between two flight paths (CT1 Q1)

\(\mathbf{a} = 3\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}+6\mathbf{k}\), \(\mathbf{b} = 2\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}+5\mathbf{k}\).

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 6 - 4 + 30 = 32, \qquad |\mathbf{a}| = 7, \qquad |\mathbf{b}| = \sqrt{33} $$
$$ \cos\theta = \frac{32}{7\sqrt{33}} \approx 0.7958 \;\Longrightarrow\; \theta \approx 37.27^\circ $$

Cross Product — "give me something perpendicular"

Produces a vector perpendicular to both inputs.

$$ \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} $$
$$ = (a_2b_3 - a_3b_2)\mathbf{i} - (a_1b_3 - a_3b_1)\mathbf{j} + (a_1b_2 - a_2b_1)\mathbf{k} $$
$$ |\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}| = |\mathbf{a}|\,|\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta $$
⚠ The \(\mathbf{j}\) term carries a minus sign. This is the single most common exam error.
What the magnitude means geometrically
QuantityFormula
Area of parallelogram on \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\)\(|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|\)
Area of triangle \(ABC\)\(\tfrac{1}{2}\big|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\big|\)
Normal to a surface through \(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}\)\(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}\)
Parallel vectors\(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}\)
Worked example — area of a triangular park (CT1 Q4)

\(A(1,2,1)\), \(B(4,3,5)\), \(C(2,7,4)\).

$$ \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 3,1,4\rangle, \qquad \overrightarrow{AC} = \langle 1,5,3\rangle $$
$$ \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} = \langle -17,\,-5,\,14\rangle, \qquad \big|\cdot\big| = \sqrt{510} \approx 22.58 $$
$$ \text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2}(22.58) \approx 11.29 \text{ square units} $$

Projections

"How much of \(\mathbf{a}\) points along \(\mathbf{b}\)?"

$$ \text{comp}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{b}|} \quad(\text{a scalar}) $$
$$ \text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{b}|^2}\right)\mathbf{b} \quad(\text{a vector}) $$
Scalar version divides by \(|\mathbf{b}|\); vector version divides by \(|\mathbf{b}|^2\) and keeps the \(\mathbf{b}\).
Worked example — wind along a runway (CT1 Q7)

\(\mathbf{W} = \langle 6,4,2\rangle\), \(\mathbf{R} = \langle 3,4,0\rangle\). \(\mathbf{W}\cdot\mathbf{R} = 34\), \(|\mathbf{R}| = 5\).

$$ \text{proj}_{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{W} = \tfrac{34}{25}\langle 3,4,0\rangle = \langle 4.08,\,5.44,\,0\rangle $$
$$ \text{comp}_{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{W} = \tfrac{34}{5} = 6.8 $$

Part 2 — Triple Products CT2

One Formula, Two Questions

$$ \mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} $$
Volume of parallelepiped \(= \big|\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c})\big|\)  ·  Coplanar \(\iff \mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = 0\)

Why they're the same formula: coplanar vectors lie flat in one plane, so the box they span is squashed — zero volume.

⚠ Volume takes the modulus. A negative triple product only means the vectors form a left-handed set.
Worked example — volume of a container (CT2 Q3)

\(\mathbf{P}\langle 4,0,1\rangle\), \(\mathbf{Q}\langle 1,3,0\rangle\), \(\mathbf{R}\langle 0,1,2\rangle\) in metres.

$$ \mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{R} = \langle 6,-2,1\rangle, \qquad \mathbf{P}\cdot(\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{R}) = 24 + 0 + 1 = 25 $$
$$ V = 25 \text{ m}^3 $$

Full solutions to all 11 CT2 questions are on the CT2 review page.

Part 3 — Lines in 3-Space Unit 1

Three Ways to Write One Line

A line needs one point \(\mathbf{a}\) and one direction \(\mathbf{b}\).

FormEquation
Vector\(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + \lambda\mathbf{b}\)
Parametric\(x = a_1 + \lambda b_1,\quad y = a_2 + \lambda b_2,\quad z = a_3 + \lambda b_3\)
Symmetric\(\dfrac{x-a_1}{b_1} = \dfrac{y-a_2}{b_2} = \dfrac{z-a_3}{b_3}\)

Through two points \(A\) and \(B\): take \(\mathbf{a} = A\) and \(\mathbf{b} = B - A\).

⚠ Symmetric form needs all three direction components non-zero. If \(b_2 = 0\), write that piece separately as \(y = a_2\).
Worked example — drone flight path (CT1 Q5)

\(P(2,-1,4)\), direction \(\mathbf{d} = 3\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k}\).

$$ \mathbf{r} = (2\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}+4\mathbf{k}) + \lambda(3\mathbf{i}-2\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{k}) $$
$$ x = 2+3\lambda,\quad y = -1-2\lambda,\quad z = 4+\lambda $$
$$ \frac{x-2}{3} = \frac{y+1}{-2} = \frac{z-4}{1} $$

Part 4 — Planes Unit 1 · Unit 3 · CT2

Three Ways to Write One Plane

A plane needs one point and either two in-plane directions or one normal.

FormEquation
Vector (parametric)\(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} + s\mathbf{u} + t\mathbf{v}\)
Vector (normal)\((\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{n} = 0\)  or  \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}\)
Cartesian\(n_1x + n_2y + n_3z = d\),   \(d = \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{n}\)
The normal is just the coefficients: \(2x - y + 2z = 5 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{n} = \langle 2,-1,2\rangle\). Ignore the constant.
Plane through three points \(A, B, C\)
  1. \(\overrightarrow{AB} = B-A\),   \(\overrightarrow{AC} = C-A\)
  2. \(\mathbf{n} = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\)
  3. \(n_1(x-a_1) + n_2(y-a_2) + n_3(z-a_3) = 0\), then expand
  4. Verify by substituting all three points back in
Plane through \(P\), parallel to \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\mathbf{v}\)

Vector form is immediate: \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{p} + s\mathbf{u} + t\mathbf{v}\). For Cartesian, take \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\) and continue as above.

Worked example — solar panel plane (CT2 Q8)

\(A(2,1,4)\), \(B(6,2,5)\), \(C(3,5,6)\).

$$ \overrightarrow{AB} = \langle 4,1,1\rangle,\quad \overrightarrow{AC} = \langle 1,4,2\rangle,\quad \mathbf{n} = \langle -2,-7,15\rangle $$
$$ -2(x-2) - 7(y-1) + 15(z-4) = 0 $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\quad 2x + 7y - 15z + 49 = 0 $$

Angles — Cosine or Sine?

Plane ↔ Plane  →  cosine
$$ \cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{n_1}\cdot\mathbf{n_2}|}{|\mathbf{n_1}|\,|\mathbf{n_2}|} $$
Line ↔ Plane  →  sine
$$ \sin\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}|}{|\mathbf{d}|\,|\mathbf{n}|} $$
Line ↔ Line  →  cosine
$$ \cos\theta = \frac{|\mathbf{d_1}\cdot\mathbf{d_2}|}{|\mathbf{d_1}|\,|\mathbf{d_2}|} $$

How to remember: the normal sticks out of the plane, so any angle you measure against it is \(90^\circ - \theta\). Swapping cosine for sine performs that flip automatically.

⚠ Keep the absolute value on top whenever the question says acute. Without it you get the obtuse supplement.
Perpendicular and parallel conditions
RelationshipPerpendicularParallel
Plane & plane\(\mathbf{n_1}\cdot\mathbf{n_2} = 0\)\(\mathbf{n_1} = \lambda\mathbf{n_2}\)
Line & plane\(\mathbf{d} = \lambda\mathbf{n}\)\(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n} = 0\)
Line & line\(\mathbf{d_1}\cdot\mathbf{d_2} = 0\)\(\mathbf{d_1} = \lambda\mathbf{d_2}\)
⚠ Note the swap on the middle row: line ⟂ plane means the line runs along the normal; line ∥ plane means it is ⟂ to the normal.

Where a Line Meets a Plane

  1. Write the line parametrically: \(x = a_1+td_1\), \(y = a_2+td_2\), \(z = a_3+td_3\)
  2. Substitute all three into the plane equation
  3. Solve the resulting linear equation for \(t\)
  4. Put that \(t\) back into the line to get the point
For a horizontal plane \(z = c\), only the \(z\)-component matters — set \(a_3 + td_3 = c\) and solve directly.

Special cases: if \(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n} = 0\) the line is parallel to the plane — either no solution, or \(0=0\) meaning the line lies entirely in the plane.

Worked example — aircraft crossing an altitude (CT2 Q7)

\(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 100+20t,\; 50+10t,\; 500-25t\rangle\) crossing \(z = 200\).

$$ 500 - 25t = 200 \;\Longrightarrow\; t = 12 \text{ s} $$
$$ (x,y,z) = (100+240,\; 50+120,\; 200) = (340,\,170,\,200) \text{ m} $$

Distance from a Point to a Plane from slides

The distance \(PN\) is the shortest — the perpendicular — from \(P\) to the plane. The point \(N\) where it lands is the foot of the perpendicular.

$$ D = \frac{\big|Ax_1 + By_1 + Cz_1 - D_0\big|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + C^2}} $$

for the plane \(Ax+By+Cz = D_0\) and the point \((x_1,y_1,z_1)\)

⚠ Watch the sign convention. Written as \(Ax+By+Cz = D_0\) you subtract \(D_0\); written as \(Ax+By+Cz+d = 0\) you add \(d\). Same formula — put the plane in one form and stay there.
Finding the foot of the perpendicular \(N\)
  1. Build the line through \(P\) along the normal: \(\mathbf{r} = P + t\mathbf{n}\)
  2. Substitute into the plane equation and solve for \(t\)
  3. That \(t\) gives \(N\); the distance is \(|PN| = |t|\,|\mathbf{n}|\)

Part 5 — Vector-Valued Functions & Arc Length Unit 2

What a Vector-Valued Function Is from slides

A rule that takes a real number input (usually time \(t\)) and outputs a vector. It maps a single parameter to multi-dimensional coordinates, describing paths like lines, circles, and helixes.

$$ \mathbf{r}(t) = x(t)\mathbf{i} + y(t)\mathbf{j} + z(t)\mathbf{k} = \langle x(t),\,y(t),\,z(t)\rangle $$
  • \(t\) is the parameter
  • \(x(t), y(t), z(t)\) are ordinary scalar functions
  • \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) represents a point in 3-space at time \(t\)
Three equivalent forms — same function, three notations
FormWritten as
Component\(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t^2,\; \sin t,\; e^t\rangle\)
Unit-vector / position-vector\(\mathbf{r}(t) = t^2\mathbf{i} + \sin t\,\mathbf{j} + e^t\mathbf{k}\)
Parametric\(x = t^2,\quad y = \sin t,\quad z = e^t\)
If a question asks to "convert between forms", it is only asking you to rewrite — nothing is calculated.

Differentiating a Vector-Valued Function from slides

Differentiate each component separately with respect to the parameter. That is the whole rule.

$$ \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \mathbf{r}'(t) = x'(t)\mathbf{i} + y'(t)\mathbf{j} + z'(t)\mathbf{k} $$
The six rules of differentiation
#Rule
1\(\dfrac{d}{dt}\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}\)
2\(\dfrac{d}{dt}(k\mathbf{r}) = k\dfrac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}\)
3\(\dfrac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{r_1} + \mathbf{r_2}) = \mathbf{r_1}' + \mathbf{r_2}'\)
4\(\dfrac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{r_1} - \mathbf{r_2}) = \mathbf{r_1}' - \mathbf{r_2}'\)
5\(\dfrac{d}{dt}(a\mathbf{r_1} + b\mathbf{r_2}) = a\mathbf{r_1}' + b\mathbf{r_2}'\)
6\(\dfrac{d}{dt}\big(f(t)\mathbf{r}\big) = f'(t)\mathbf{r} + f(t)\mathbf{r}'\)  (chain / product rule)

Here \(\mathbf{c}\) is a constant vector, \(a, b, k\) are constants, and \(f\) is any function of \(t\).

What the derivatives mean physically
QuantityFormulaType
Position\(\mathbf{r}(t)\)vector
Velocity\(\mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{r}'(t)\)vector
Speed\(|\mathbf{v}(t)| = |\mathbf{r}'(t)|\)scalar
Acceleration\(\mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{r}''(t)\)vector
Unit tangent\(\mathbf{T}(t) = \dfrac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{|\mathbf{r}'(t)|}\)vector
Speed is the magnitude of velocity — and it is exactly the thing you integrate to get arc length.

Arc Length from slides

Arc length is the distance measured along the curve between two points — not the straight-line distance between the endpoints.

Where the formula comes from

For a small change in \(t\), the displacement is \(d\mathbf{r} = (dx, dy, dz)\), so the small distance along the curve is

$$ ds = \sqrt{[x'(t)]^2 + [y'(t)]^2 + [z'(t)]^2}\;dt $$

Adding up every \(ds\) from \(t=a\) to \(t=b\) gives

$$ \boxed{\;L = \int_a^b \sqrt{[x'(t)]^2 + [y'(t)]^2 + [z'(t)]^2}\;dt = \int_a^b |\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt\;} $$
The integrand is the speed. Every arc-length problem is: differentiate → take the magnitude → integrate.
The method, every time
  1. Differentiate componentwise to get \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\)
  2. Compute \(|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\) and simplify before integrating
  3. Integrate over the given range of \(t\)
⚠ Step 2 is where the marks are. In almost every exam problem the \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\) identity collapses the square root to a constant, turning a frightening integral into \(\int_a^b(\text{number})\,dt\). If your square root doesn't simplify, re-check the differentiation.
Worked example 1 — circular motion from slides

\(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 4\cos t,\; 4\sin t\rangle\),   \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\).

$$ \mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -4\sin t,\; 4\cos t\rangle $$
$$ |\mathbf{r}'(t)| = \sqrt{16\sin^2 t + 16\cos^2 t} $$
$$ = \sqrt{16(\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t)} = \sqrt{16} = 4 $$
$$ L = \int_0^{2\pi} 4\,dt = 4\big[t\big]_0^{2\pi} = 8\pi \approx 25.13 $$
Sanity check: this is a circle of radius 4, and its circumference is \(2\pi r = 8\pi\). ✓
Worked example 2 — helix from slides

\(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 3\cos t,\; 3\sin t,\; 4t\rangle\),   \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\).

$$ \mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle -3\sin t,\; 3\cos t,\; 4\rangle $$
$$ |\mathbf{r}'(t)| = \sqrt{9\sin^2 t + 9\cos^2 t + 16} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5 $$
$$ L = \int_0^{2\pi} 5\,dt = 5\big[t\big]_0^{2\pi} = 10\pi \approx 31.42 $$

Note the pattern: for a helix \(\langle a\cos t,\, a\sin t,\, ct\rangle\) the speed is always the constant \(\sqrt{a^2 + c^2}\), so \(L = \sqrt{a^2+c^2}\,(b-a_{\text{start}})\).

The one integral worth memorising from slides

If the square root does not collapse, the slides give you the standard result:

$$ \int \sqrt{x^2 + a^2}\;dx = \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{x^2+a^2} + \frac{a^2}{2}\ln\!\left(x + \sqrt{x^2+a^2}\right) + c $$

Part 6 — Multiple Integrals Unit 3

The Big Picture from slides

IntegralAdds up quantities…Gives you
Single \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\)along a lineArea under a curve
Double \(\displaystyle\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA\)over an areaVolume under a surface
Triple \(\displaystyle\iiint_V f(x,y,z)\,dV\)throughout a volumeMass, charge, or volume itself
Each step up adds one dimension of "sweeping". The mechanics never change: integrate one variable at a time, innermost first.

Double Integrals from slides

For a surface \(z = f(x,y)\) above a region \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane, the double integral

$$ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA $$

represents the volume under the surface and above the region \(R\).

Over a rectangle \(R = [a,b]\times[c,d]\)
$$ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = \int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy $$
Fubini's theorem: over a rectangle with constant limits, the order does not matter — you get the same answer either way. Pick whichever order integrates more easily.
How to evaluate an iterated integral
  1. Work innermost first — match the integral sign to its \(dy\) or \(dx\)
  2. While integrating with respect to \(y\), treat \(x\) as a constant (and vice versa)
  3. Substitute the inner limits — you now have a single-variable function
  4. Integrate that with respect to the outer variable and substitute its limits
⚠ The limits pair with the differentials from the inside out: \(\int_a^b\!\int_c^d f\,dy\,dx\) means \(y\) runs \(c \to d\) and \(x\) runs \(a \to b\).
Example 1 — volume under a plane from slides

Find the volume under \(z = x + y\) over the rectangle \(0 \le x \le 1\), \(0 \le y \le 2\); that is, \(R = [0,1]\times[0,2]\).

$$ V = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2 (x+y)\,dy\,dx $$

Inner integral (treat \(x\) as constant):

$$ \int_0^2 (x+y)\,dy = \left[xy + \frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^2 = 2x + 2 $$

Outer integral:

$$ V = \int_0^1 (2x+2)\,dx = \big[x^2 + 2x\big]_0^1 = 1 + 2 = 3 $$
\(V = 3\) cubic units
Example 2 — order doesn't matter from slides

(a) \(\displaystyle\int_1^3\!\!\int_2^4 (40 - 2xy)\,dy\,dx\)

$$ \int_2^4 (40-2xy)\,dy = \big[40y - xy^2\big]_2^4 $$
$$ = (160 - 16x) - (80 - 4x) = 80 - 12x $$
$$ \int_1^3 (80 - 12x)\,dx = \big[80x - 6x^2\big]_1^3 $$
$$ = (240 - 54) - (80 - 6) = 112 $$

(b) \(\displaystyle\int_2^4\!\!\int_1^3 (40 - 2xy)\,dx\,dy\)

$$ \int_1^3 (40-2xy)\,dx = \big[40x - x^2y\big]_1^3 $$
$$ = (120 - 9y) - (40 - y) = 80 - 8y $$
$$ \int_2^4 (80 - 8y)\,dy = \big[80y - 4y^2\big]_2^4 $$
$$ = (320 - 64) - (160 - 16) = 112 $$
Both give 112 — this is Fubini's theorem in action.
Over a general (non-rectangular) region

When the region is bounded by curves, the inner limits become functions and the outer limits must be constants.

$$ \int_a^b\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx \qquad\text{or}\qquad \int_c^d\!\!\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy $$
⚠ The outer limits are always numbers. If a variable survives to your final answer, your limits are in the wrong order.

Setting \(f = 1\) gives the plain area of the region: \(\text{Area} = \iint_R dA\).

Triple Integrals from slides

A triple integral adds up infinitely many tiny quantities throughout a three-dimensional region (a solid). A double integral works over an area; a triple integral works over a volume.

$$ \iiint_V f(x,y,z)\,dV, \qquad dV = dx\,dy\,dz $$
The special case that shows up most
Setting \(f(x,y,z) = 1\) makes the triple integral give the volume of the solid: \(V = \iiint_V 1\,dV\).
Example — a rectangular box from slides

Suppose \(0 \le x \le 2\), \(0 \le y \le 3\), \(0 \le z \le 4\) — a box with dimensions \(2\times3\times4\).

$$ V = \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^3\!\!\int_0^4 1\;dz\,dy\,dx $$

Innermost: \(\displaystyle\int_0^4 1\,dz = \big[z\big]_0^4 = 4\)

Middle: \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 4\,dy = \big[4y\big]_0^3 = 12\)

Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 12\,dx = \big[12x\big]_0^2 = 24\)

\(V = 24\) cubic units — which is just \(2 \times 3 \times 4\), exactly as expected. ✓

That check is the point of the example: when all six limits are constants, a triple integral of 1 is simply the product of the three side lengths. Use it to verify your setup before attempting harder solids.

Part 7 — Exam Strategy & Common Mistakes

Reading the question — what to reach for

The question says…Reach for
"angle between" two vectorsDot product, \(\cos\theta\)
"area of parallelogram / triangle"Cross product (halve it for a triangle)
"perpendicular to both" / "normal"Cross product
"volume of parallelepiped / container"Scalar triple product, take modulus
"are they coplanar?"Scalar triple product \(= 0\)?
"equation of the plane through 3 points"\(\mathbf{n} = \overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\)
"where does the line cross"Substitute parametric line into the plane
"shortest distance from a point to a plane"\(\frac{|Ax_1+By_1+Cz_1-D|}{\sqrt{A^2+B^2+C^2}}\)
"how far does it travel along the path"Arc length, \(\int|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt\)
"how fast" at an instantSpeed, \(|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\) — no integral
"volume under the surface"Double integral of \(f\)
"volume of the solid"Triple integral of 1

The mistakes that actually cost marks

  1. The \(\mathbf{j}\) minus sign in every cross product determinant.
  2. Forgetting the modulus — on volume, and on the dot product inside angle formulas.
  3. Sine vs cosine — sine for line–plane, cosine for plane–plane and line–line.
  4. Wrong subtraction order — \(\overrightarrow{AB} = B - A\).
  5. Halving, or not halving — triangles need the \(\tfrac{1}{2}\); parallelograms don't.
  6. Not simplifying \(|\mathbf{r}'(t)|\) before integrating — use \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\).
  7. Mismatched integral limits — innermost limits belong to the innermost differential.
  8. A variable left in a definite-integral answer — a sure sign the limits are wrong.
  9. Dropping units — m³ for a container, seconds for a time, "square units" for an area.

Checks that take ten seconds and save whole questions

  • Substitute every given point back into a plane equation you derived.
  • Substitute your intersection point into the plane equation.
  • Arc length of a circle should equal \(2\pi r\); of a box, the product of its sides.
  • Volumes, areas, lengths, and distances are never negative.
  • An acute angle must come out below \(90^\circ\) — if it doesn't, you dropped a modulus.
  • A normal can be scaled down by a common factor: \(\langle 2,2,4\rangle \to \langle 1,1,2\rangle\).
