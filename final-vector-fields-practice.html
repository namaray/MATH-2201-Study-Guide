Scalar & Vector Fields (Q1–Q4)

A field is a rule that attaches something to every point in space. If it attaches a number, it's a scalar field. If it attaches an arrow (a vector), it's a vector field. These four questions just ask you to recognise which is which and read off values.

Q1. Temperature in a Metal Block Scalar Field

\(T(x,y,z) = 4x^2 + y^2 + 3z\). (a) Explain why \(T\) represents a scalar field. (b) Find the temperature at \((1, -2, 3)\).
Formula you need A scalar field is a function \(f:\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}\) — three inputs, one number out. To find its value at a point, substitute the coordinates.
The idea Imagine pushing a thermometer into the block at any spot. You read off one number. A thermometer doesn't point anywhere — temperature has no direction. Compare that with wind: to describe wind you need a speed and a direction, so wind is a vector field. Temperature isn't.
(a) Why it's a scalar field

For every point \((x,y,z)\) in the block, the formula \(4x^2 + y^2 + 3z\) produces exactly one real number — the temperature there. There's magnitude but no direction, so \(T\) is a scalar field.

(b) Substitute the point

Put \(x = 1\), \(y = -2\), \(z = 3\) into the formula. Watch the sign: \((-2)^2\) is \(+4\), because squaring removes the minus.

$$ T(1,-2,3) = 4(1)^2 + (-2)^2 + 3(3) $$
$$ = 4 + 4 + 9 = 17 $$
(a) It assigns one number (no direction) to each point  ·  (b) \(T(1,-2,3) = 17\)
What it means The thermometer at \((1,-2,3)\) reads 17. Notice the \(4x^2\) term: moving in the \(x\)-direction changes the temperature four times faster than the same move in \(y\) — that's the kind of thing the gradient (Q5–Q6) will measure precisely.

Q2. Chemical Concentration Scalar Field

\(C(x,y,z) = x^2 + 2y^2 + z^2 - 4x\). (a) Find \(C(2,1,-1)\). (b) Describe what information \(C\) provides at each point in space.
Formula you need Evaluate by substitution. For part (b): points where \(C\) has the same value form a level surface \(C(x,y,z) = k\).
The idea Like Q1, but now the number is "how much chemical is here". It tells you how much, never which way.
(a) Substitute
$$ C(2,1,-1) = (2)^2 + 2(1)^2 + (-1)^2 - 4(2) $$
$$ = 4 + 2 + 1 - 8 = -1 $$
(a) \(C(2,1,-1) = -1\)
A real concentration can't be negative, so treat \(-1\) as the model's output (for example, measured relative to some reference level). Mathematically, the answer is \(-1\).
(b) What \(C\) tells you — understanding the shape

To see the structure of \(C\), complete the square in \(x\) (the same trick as conic sections):

$$ x^2 - 4x = (x - 2)^2 - 4 $$
$$ C = (x-2)^2 + 2y^2 + z^2 - 4 $$

Now everything is clear:

  • At each point, \(C\) gives one number — the concentration there.
  • The squared terms are never negative, so \(C\) is smallest at \((2,0,0)\), where it equals \(-4\).
  • Moving away from \((2,0,0)\) in any direction increases \(C\). Surfaces of equal concentration, \(C = k\), are ellipsoids centred at \((2,0,0)\).
What it means \(C\) is a map of "how much chemical, where". It doesn't say which way the chemical flows — but its gradient \(\nabla C\) would point away from \((2,0,0)\), the direction concentration increases fastest.

Q3. Fluid Velocity Field Vector Field

\(\mathbf{V} = (y + z)\mathbf{i} + (x - z)\mathbf{j} + (x + y)\mathbf{k}\). (a) Find the velocity at \(P(1,2,-1)\). (b) Find its magnitude.
Formula you need Evaluate each component at the point. Then
$$ |\mathbf{V}| = \sqrt{V_1^2 + V_2^2 + V_3^2} $$
The idea Picture a tiny cork floating in the water at \(P\). The vector field tells you the cork's velocity — which way it's carried and how fast. The magnitude of that arrow is the speed.
Step 1 — Evaluate each component separately

Each component is its own little formula. Do them one at a time with \(x = 1\), \(y = 2\), \(z = -1\):

$$ y + z = 2 + (-1) = 1 $$
$$ x - z = 1 - (-1) = 2 $$
$$ x + y = 1 + 2 = 3 $$
$$ \mathbf{V}(1,2,-1) = \mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j} + 3\mathbf{k} $$
Step 2 — Magnitude

Length of a 3D arrow is Pythagoras in three dimensions:

$$ |\mathbf{V}| = \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{14} $$
(a) \(\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j} + 3\mathbf{k}\)  ·  (b) \(|\mathbf{V}| = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.742\)
What it means At \(P\), the fluid moves at speed \(\approx 3.74\), mostly upward (the \(\mathbf{k}\)-part is largest). Its direction of travel is the unit vector \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{14}}\langle 1,2,3\rangle \approx \langle 0.27, 0.53, 0.80\rangle\).
The classic slip is \(x - z = 1 - 1 = 0\). Subtracting a negative adds: \(1 - (-1) = 2\).

Q4. Force Field Vector Field

\(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (3x^2 - 2y)\mathbf{i} + (4y + x)\mathbf{j}\). (a) Find \(\mathbf{F}(2,-1)\). (b) Find the magnitude and direction of the force there.
Formula you need For \(\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j}\):
$$ |\mathbf{F}| = \sqrt{P^2 + Q^2} $$
$$ \hat{\mathbf{F}} = \frac{\mathbf{F}}{|\mathbf{F}|} $$
$$ \tan\theta = \frac{Q}{P} $$
(Choose the quadrant of \(\theta\) from the signs of \(P\) and \(Q\).)
The idea A force is a push. "Magnitude" = how hard it pushes. "Direction" = which way it pushes. You can give direction either as a unit vector or as an angle from the positive \(x\)-axis — giving both is safest.
(a) Evaluate at (2, −1)
$$ P = 3(2)^2 - 2(-1) = 12 + 2 = 14 $$
$$ Q = 4(-1) + 2 = -2 $$
$$ \mathbf{F}(2,-1) = 14\mathbf{i} - 2\mathbf{j} $$
(b) Magnitude
$$ |\mathbf{F}| = \sqrt{14^2 + (-2)^2} $$
$$ = \sqrt{200} = 10\sqrt2 \approx 14.14 $$
(b) Direction

Unit vector: divide the force by its length, so the result has length 1 and only carries direction.

$$ \hat{\mathbf{F}} = \frac{14\mathbf{i} - 2\mathbf{j}}{10\sqrt2} = \frac{7\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j}}{5\sqrt2} $$
$$ \hat{\mathbf{F}} \approx 0.990\,\mathbf{i} - 0.141\,\mathbf{j} $$

Angle: \(P > 0\) and \(Q < 0\), so the arrow is in Quadrant IV — just below the positive \(x\)-axis.

$$ \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{-2}{14}\right) \approx -8.13^\circ \;\;(\text{or } 351.87^\circ) $$
(a) \(14\mathbf{i} - 2\mathbf{j}\)  ·  (b) \(|\mathbf{F}| = 10\sqrt2 \approx 14.14\), pointing \(8.13^\circ\) below the positive \(x\)-axis
What it means At \((2,-1)\) the force pushes almost straight in the \(+x\) direction (14 units right, only 2 down), with strength about 14.14.

Gradient & Directional Derivatives (Q5–Q8)

The gradient turns a scalar field into a vector field: at each point it points the way the function increases fastest, and its length is that fastest rate. The directional derivative asks a narrower question: how fast does the function change if I move in this particular direction?

Q5. Gradient Gradient

\(\phi(x,y,z) = x^2y + 2yz^2 + 3x\). (a) Find \(\nabla\phi\). (b) Evaluate it at \((1, 2, -1)\).
Formula you need
$$ \nabla\phi = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\mathbf{k} $$
A partial derivative \(\partial/\partial x\) means: differentiate with respect to \(x\), treating \(y\) and \(z\) as constants.
The idea Each partial derivative measures the slope of \(\phi\) along one axis. The gradient bundles the three slopes into one arrow. That arrow turns out to point in the direction of steepest increase.
Step 1 — \(\partial\phi/\partial x\) (treat \(y, z\) as constants)

\(x^2y\): \(y\) is a constant multiplier, so this gives \(2xy\).   \(2yz^2\): no \(x\) at all, so it's a constant — derivative 0.   \(3x\) gives 3.

$$ \phi_x = 2xy + 3 $$
Step 2 — \(\partial\phi/\partial y\) (treat \(x, z\) as constants)

\(x^2y \to x^2\).   \(2yz^2 \to 2z^2\).   \(3x\) has no \(y\), so 0.

$$ \phi_y = x^2 + 2z^2 $$
Step 3 — \(\partial\phi/\partial z\) (treat \(x, y\) as constants)

Only \(2yz^2\) contains \(z\): it gives \(2y \cdot 2z = 4yz\).

$$ \phi_z = 4yz $$
Step 4 — Assemble, then substitute the point

Always find the general gradient first, then plug in. If you plug in first, you're differentiating a number and get zero.

$$ \nabla\phi = (2xy + 3)\mathbf{i} + (x^2 + 2z^2)\mathbf{j} + 4yz\,\mathbf{k} $$
$$ \nabla\phi(1,2,-1) = (4 + 3)\mathbf{i} + (1 + 2)\mathbf{j} + (-8)\mathbf{k} $$
(a) \((2xy + 3)\mathbf{i} + (x^2 + 2z^2)\mathbf{j} + 4yz\,\mathbf{k}\)  ·  (b) \(7\mathbf{i} + 3\mathbf{j} - 8\mathbf{k}\)
What it means Standing at \((1,2,-1)\), \(\phi\) increases fastest if you head in the direction \(\langle 7, 3, -8\rangle\), and the rate in that direction is \(|\nabla\phi| = \sqrt{49 + 9 + 64} = \sqrt{122} \approx 11.05\).

Q6. Gradient of an Elevation Gradient

\(h(x,y) = x^2 + 4y^2 - 2xy\). (a) Find \(\nabla h\). (b) Find the gradient at \((2,1)\). (c) What does its direction indicate geometrically?
Formula you need
$$ \nabla h = \langle h_x,\ h_y\rangle $$
$$ \text{steepest slope} = |\nabla h| $$
The idea \(h\) is the height of a hill above the point \((x,y)\) on a map. The gradient is a compass needle printed on the map: it points the way to walk if you want to climb as steeply as possible.
(a) Partial derivatives

For \(h_x\), \(4y^2\) is constant (gives 0), and \(-2xy\) gives \(-2y\). For \(h_y\), \(x^2\) is constant, \(4y^2\) gives \(8y\), and \(-2xy\) gives \(-2x\).

$$ \nabla h = (2x - 2y)\mathbf{i} + (8y - 2x)\mathbf{j} $$
(b) At (2, 1)
$$ \nabla h(2,1) = (4 - 2)\mathbf{i} + (8 - 4)\mathbf{j} = 2\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j} $$
(a) \((2x - 2y)\mathbf{i} + (8y - 2x)\mathbf{j}\)  ·  (b) \(2\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j}\)
(c) What the direction means — and why
  • Steepest ascent. \(2\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j}\) is the direction of straight uphill from \((2,1)\) — about \(63.4^\circ\) from the \(x\)-axis.
  • Perpendicular to the contour. Walking along a contour line keeps the height constant, so the rate of change in that direction is zero. Since the rate in direction \(\mathbf{u}\) is \(\nabla h\cdot\mathbf{u}\), zero rate means \(\mathbf{u} \perp \nabla h\). So the gradient is always at right angles to the contour.
  • Its length is the steepness. \(|\nabla h| = \sqrt{4 + 16} = 2\sqrt5 \approx 4.47\): height rises about 4.47 units per unit of horizontal distance in that direction.
What it means On a hiking map, the gradient arrow at any point crosses the contour lines at right angles, pointing uphill. Water would flow the opposite way, along \(-\nabla h\).

Q7. Directional Derivative Directional Derivative

\(f(x,y) = x^2y + 3y^2\). Find the directional derivative at \(P(1,2)\) in the direction of \(\mathbf{v} = 3\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j}\).
Formula you need
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{u} $$
$$ \mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{|\mathbf{v}|} $$
The idea The gradient holds the complete slope information at a point. Dotting it with a direction picks out "how much of that slope you feel walking this way". The direction must be a unit vector because we only care about which way you walk, not how long the arrow \(\mathbf{v}\) happens to be. Without dividing by \(|\mathbf{v}|\), doubling \(\mathbf{v}\) would double the answer — which makes no sense.
Step 1 — Gradient, then evaluate at P
$$ \nabla f = \langle 2xy,\ x^2 + 6y\rangle $$
$$ \nabla f(1,2) = \langle 4,\ 13\rangle $$
Step 2 — Make the direction a unit vector
$$ |\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5 $$
$$ \mathbf{u} = \left\langle \tfrac35,\ \tfrac45\right\rangle $$
Step 3 — Dot product
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = 4\cdot\tfrac35 + 13\cdot\tfrac45 = \frac{12 + 52}{5} = \frac{64}{5} $$
\(D_{\mathbf{u}}f(1,2) = \dfrac{64}{5} = 12.8\)
What it means Moving from \((1,2)\) in the direction \(\langle 3,4\rangle\), \(f\) increases by about 12.8 per unit of distance travelled. Sanity check: the maximum possible rate is \(|\nabla f| = \sqrt{185} \approx 13.6\). Our 12.8 is just below that, because \(\langle 3,4\rangle\) is only about \(20^\circ\) away from the gradient direction. A directional derivative can never be bigger than \(|\nabla f|\) — if you'd forgotten to divide by 5 you'd get 64, which is impossible.

Q8. Rate of Change Toward a Point Directional Derivative

\(T(x,y,z) = x^2 + 2y^2 + 3z^2\). Find the rate of change at \(P(1,-1,2)\) in the direction from \(P\) toward \(Q(3,2,3)\).
Formula you need
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}T = \nabla T\cdot\mathbf{u} $$
$$ \mathbf{u} = \frac{\overrightarrow{PQ}}{|\overrightarrow{PQ}|} $$
$$ \overrightarrow{PQ} = Q - P $$
The idea Same as Q7 in 3D. The only new part is building the direction: "from \(P\) toward \(Q\)" means the arrow \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\) — head minus tail, exactly as in the midterm.
Step 1 — Gradient at P
$$ \nabla T = \langle 2x,\ 4y,\ 6z\rangle $$
$$ \nabla T(1,-1,2) = \langle 2,\ -4,\ 12\rangle $$
Step 2 — Direction from P to Q, made unit
$$ \overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle 3-1,\ 2-(-1),\ 3-2\rangle = \langle 2,\ 3,\ 1\rangle $$
$$ |\overrightarrow{PQ}| = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 1} = \sqrt{14} $$
Step 3 — Dot product
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}T = \frac{2(2) + (-4)(3) + 12(1)}{\sqrt{14}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{14}} $$
\(D_{\mathbf{u}}T = \dfrac{4}{\sqrt{14}} = \dfrac{2\sqrt{14}}{7} \approx 1.069\)
What it means Heading toward \(Q\), the temperature rises slowly — about 1.07 per unit distance. Compare with the fastest possible rate here, \(|\nabla T| = \sqrt{164} \approx 12.8\). The direction toward \(Q\) is about \(85^\circ\) away from the gradient, so it's almost "sideways" across the temperature contours — very little of the steepness is felt. That's exactly what \(D_{\mathbf{u}}T = |\nabla T|\cos\theta\) predicts.

Potential Functions & Conservative Fields (Q9–Q12)

A vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative if it is the gradient of some scalar function \(\phi\) (the potential): \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla\phi\). These questions run the gradient backwards: given the arrows, can you find the "height function" they're the slopes of? First you test whether such a \(\phi\) can exist; then you build it.

Q9. Does a Potential Exist? Conservative Field

\(\mathbf{F} = (2xy + 3)\mathbf{i} + x^2\mathbf{j} + 4z\mathbf{k}\). Determine whether a potential \(\phi\) exists; if so, find \(\phi\) with \(\nabla\phi = \mathbf{F}\).
Formula you need \(\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}\) has a potential (on all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) exactly when \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\):
$$ R_y = Q_z $$
$$ P_z = R_x $$
$$ Q_x = P_y $$
The idea — why this test works If \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla\phi\), then \(P = \phi_x\) and \(Q = \phi_y\). So \(P_y = \phi_{xy}\) and \(Q_x = \phi_{yx}\). For any smooth function, the order of mixed partial derivatives doesn't matter (\(\phi_{xy} = \phi_{yx}\)), so a gradient field must have \(P_y = Q_x\) — and similarly for the other pairs. That's all "curl = 0" is saying. If any pair fails, no \(\phi\) can exist.
Step 1 — Identify P, Q, R
$$ P = 2xy + 3 $$
$$ Q = x^2 $$
$$ R = 4z $$
Step 2 — Check the three cross-derivative pairs
$$ R_y = 0 = Q_z $$
$$ P_z = 0 = R_x $$
$$ Q_x = 2x = P_y $$

All three match, so \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\): a potential exists.

Step 3 — Build φ from P

We need \(\phi_x = P\), so integrate \(P\) with respect to \(x\). The "constant of integration" can be any function of \(y\) and \(z\), because differentiating with respect to \(x\) would wipe it out anyway.

$$ \phi = \int (2xy + 3)\,dx = x^2y + 3x + g(y,z) $$
Step 4 — Use Q to pin down g

Differentiate our \(\phi\) with respect to \(y\) and demand it equals \(Q = x^2\):

$$ \phi_y = x^2 + g_y = x^2 $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g_y = 0 $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g = h(z) $$

So \(g\) doesn't depend on \(y\) at all — only possibly on \(z\).

Step 5 — Use R to pin down h
$$ \phi_z = h'(z) = 4z $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; h = 2z^2 + C $$
Yes, \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative: \(\phi = x^2y + 3x + 2z^2 + C\)
What it means — and how to check \(\mathbf{F}\) is the "slope field" of the function \(x^2y + 3x + 2z^2\). Check by differentiating: \(\phi_x = 2xy + 3\) ✓, \(\phi_y = x^2\) ✓, \(\phi_z = 4z\) ✓. Always do this check — it takes ten seconds.

Q10. Recover \(\phi\) From Its Gradient Potential Function

\(\nabla\phi = (2x + yz)\mathbf{i} + (xz + 2y)\mathbf{j} + xy\,\mathbf{k}\). Find \(\phi(x,y,z)\), including the arbitrary constant.
Formula you need
$$ \phi_x = P, \quad \phi_y = Q, \quad \phi_z = R $$
Integrate one, then use the others to find the "constant" functions.
The idea This is antidifferentiation in three variables. Here you're told it's a gradient, so a potential definitely exists — you just have to build it. Why "+C"? Because adding any constant to \(\phi\) doesn't change its gradient (the derivative of a constant is zero), just like \(\int 2x\,dx = x^2 + C\).
Step 1 — Integrate P with respect to x
$$ \phi = \int (2x + yz)\,dx = x^2 + xyz + g(y,z) $$
Step 2 — Match with Q
$$ \phi_y = xz + g_y = xz + 2y $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g_y = 2y $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g = y^2 + h(z) $$
Step 3 — Match with R
$$ \phi_z = xy + h'(z) = xy $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; h'(z) = 0 $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; h = C $$
\(\phi(x,y,z) = x^2 + y^2 + xyz + C\)
What it means Check: \(\nabla(x^2 + y^2 + xyz) = \langle 2x + yz,\ 2y + xz,\ xy\rangle\) ✓. Notice why we didn't just integrate each component and add: \(\int P\,dx\) and \(\int Q\,dy\) both contain \(xyz\), so adding them would count it twice. Going through \(g(y,z)\) and \(h(z)\) only adds what's genuinely new.

Q11. Conservative on \(\mathbb{R}^2\)? Conservative Field

\(\mathbf{F} = (y\cos x + 2x)\mathbf{i} + (\sin x + 2y)\mathbf{j}\). Is it conservative on \(\mathbb{R}^2\)? If so, find its potential.
Formula you need In 2D, \(\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j}\) is conservative on a region with no holes when
$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} $$
The idea Same mixed-partials reason as Q9, with only one pair to check. "On \(\mathbb{R}^2\)" matters: the whole plane has no holes (it's simply connected), and on such a region the test is enough to guarantee a potential exists.
Step 1 — The test

\(P = y\cos x + 2x\): differentiate in \(y\) (treat \(x\) as constant) → \(\cos x\).   \(Q = \sin x + 2y\): differentiate in \(x\) → \(\cos x\).

$$ P_y = \cos x = Q_x $$

Equal everywhere on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) → conservative.

Step 2 — Integrate P with respect to x

\(\int y\cos x\,dx = y\sin x\) (with \(y\) a constant), and \(\int 2x\,dx = x^2\).

$$ \phi = y\sin x + x^2 + g(y) $$
Step 3 — Match with Q
$$ \phi_y = \sin x + g'(y) = \sin x + 2y $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g'(y) = 2y $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g = y^2 $$
Conservative: \(\phi = y\sin x + x^2 + y^2 + C\)
What it means Any line integral of this \(\mathbf{F}\) between two points equals the change in \(y\sin x + x^2 + y^2\) between them, whatever path you take.

Q12. Conservative in 3D? Conservative Field

\(\mathbf{F} = (2x + yz)\mathbf{i} + (xz + 2y)\mathbf{j} + (xy + 2z)\mathbf{k}\). (a) Is \(\mathbf{F}\) conservative? (b) If so, find \(\phi\).
Formula you need
$$ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = (R_y - Q_z)\,\mathbf{i} $$
$$ + (P_z - R_x)\,\mathbf{j} + (Q_x - P_y)\,\mathbf{k} $$
Conservative \(\iff\) all three components are zero.
The idea Exactly Q9's method. Bonus insight: this field is Q10's field with an extra \(2z\,\mathbf{k}\), so expect the same potential plus one extra term.
(a) Curl test
$$ R_y - Q_z = x - x = 0 $$
$$ P_z - R_x = y - y = 0 $$
$$ Q_x - P_y = z - z = 0 $$

\(\nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\) → conservative.

(b) Build φ
$$ \phi = \int (2x + yz)\,dx = x^2 + xyz + g(y,z) $$
$$ \phi_y = xz + g_y = xz + 2y $$
$$ \Rightarrow\; g = y^2 + h(z) $$
$$ \phi_z = xy + h'(z) = xy + 2z $$
$$ \Rightarrow\; h = z^2 $$
(a) Yes  ·  (b) \(\phi = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + xyz + C\)
What it means As predicted: Q10's potential \(x^2 + y^2 + xyz\) plus \(z^2\), which is what the extra \(2z\,\mathbf{k}\) contributes (\(\partial(z^2)/\partial z = 2z\)).

Line Integrals (Q13–Q14)

\(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) adds up "how much the field pushes along the path" over every tiny step \(d\mathbf{r}\) of a curve \(C\). If \(\mathbf{F}\) is a force, that total is the work done. The first thing to ask is always: is the field conservative? If it is, the answer only depends on the endpoints.

Q13. Line Integral Along a Straight Path Line Integral

Evaluate \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) where \(\mathbf{F} = (y + 2x)\mathbf{i} + (x + z)\mathbf{j} + y\,\mathbf{k}\) and \(C\) is the straight line from \(A(1,0,0)\) to \(B(2,1,2)\).
Formulas you need
$$ \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}\big(\mathbf{r}(t)\big)\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt $$
$$ \text{Straight line: } \mathbf{r}(t) = A + t(B - A) $$
$$ 0 \le t \le 1 $$
$$ \text{If } \mathbf{F} = \nabla\phi:\quad \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(B) - \phi(A) $$
The idea At each moment, \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt\) is "the part of the force along your direction of motion × the tiny distance moved". Adding these up gives the total work. If the field is conservative, it's like lifting something in gravity: the work depends only on where you start and finish, not the route — so you can skip the integral and use \(\phi(B) - \phi(A)\). We'll do both and see them agree.

Method 1 — Shortcut via the potential

Step 1 — Is it conservative?
$$ P = y + 2x,\quad Q = x + z,\quad R = y $$
$$ R_y - Q_z = 1 - 1 = 0 $$
$$ P_z - R_x = 0 - 0 = 0 $$
$$ Q_x - P_y = 1 - 1 = 0 $$

Yes — so the path doesn't matter.

Step 2 — Find φ
$$ \phi = \int (y + 2x)\,dx = xy + x^2 + g(y,z) $$
$$ \phi_y = x + g_y = x + z $$
$$ \Rightarrow\; g = yz + h(z) $$
$$ \phi_z = y + h'(z) = y $$
$$ \Rightarrow\; h = \text{const} $$
$$ \phi = x^2 + xy + yz $$
Step 3 — Subtract endpoint values
$$ \phi(B) = 4 + 2 + 2 = 8 $$
$$ \phi(A) = 1 + 0 + 0 = 1 $$
$$ \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 8 - 1 = 7 $$

Method 2 — Parametrise the line (works for any field)

Step 1 — Describe the path

Start at \(A\) and add a fraction \(t\) of the journey \(B - A = \langle 1, 1, 2\rangle\). At \(t = 0\) you're at \(A\); at \(t = 1\), at \(B\).

$$ \mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 1 + t,\ t,\ 2t\rangle $$
$$ \mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle 1, 1, 2\rangle $$
Step 2 — Field along the path

Substitute \(x = 1 + t\), \(y = t\), \(z = 2t\) into each component of \(\mathbf{F}\):

$$ \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \langle 3t + 2,\ 3t + 1,\ t\rangle $$
Step 3 — Dot with the velocity, then integrate
$$ \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{r}' = (3t + 2) + (3t + 1) + 2t = 8t + 3 $$
$$ \int_0^1 (8t + 3)\,dt = \Big[4t^2 + 3t\Big]_0^1 = 7 $$
\(\displaystyle\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 7\)
What it means The field does 7 units of work moving a particle from \(A\) to \(B\). Because \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative, it would also be 7 along any other path from \(A\) to \(B\). The question names a "straight-line path", so writing out Method 2 is safest in the exam — and Method 1 is a perfect check.

Q14. Work Along a Parabola Line Integral

Find the work done by \(\mathbf{F} = (x^2 - y)\mathbf{i} + (2xy + 1)\mathbf{j}\) on a particle moving along \(y = x^2\) from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\), using \(W = \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\).
Formula you need
$$ W = \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy $$
With \(x = x(t)\), \(y = y(t)\): replace \(dx = x'(t)\,dt\) and \(dy = y'(t)\,dt\).
The idea First ask: is there a shortcut? Only if the field is conservative. Here it isn't — so the work genuinely depends on the route, and we must follow the parabola step by step.
Step 1 — Check for a shortcut
$$ P_y = -1 $$
$$ Q_x = 2y $$

Not equal → not conservative. No potential exists; we have to integrate along \(y = x^2\).

Step 2 — Parametrise the curve

The simplest choice is to let \(x\) itself be the parameter: \(x = t\). Then the curve forces \(y = t^2\). As \(t\) goes from 0 to 1 we travel from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\).

$$ x = t,\quad y = t^2,\quad 0 \le t \le 1 $$

The small steps: \(dx = dt\), and since \(y = t^2\) changes at rate \(2t\), \(dy = 2t\,dt\).

$$ dx = dt $$
$$ dy = 2t\,dt $$
Step 3 — Put the field on the curve
$$ P = t^2 - t^2 = 0 $$
$$ Q = 2(t)(t^2) + 1 = 2t^3 + 1 $$

\(P\) vanishes because on this curve \(x^2\) and \(y\) are equal — the force has no horizontal push along the parabola. Not a mistake, just a nice simplification.

Step 4 — Assemble and integrate
$$ W = \int_0^1 \Big[0\cdot dt + (2t^3 + 1)(2t\,dt)\Big] $$
$$ = \int_0^1 (4t^4 + 2t)\,dt = \left[\frac{4t^5}{5} + t^2\right]_0^1 $$
$$ = \frac45 + 1 = \frac95 $$
\(W = \dfrac{9}{5} = 1.8\) units of work
What it means The force does 1.8 units of work on the particle along this parabola. Because the field isn't conservative, a different path between the same two points (say a straight line) would generally give a different answer — that's exactly what "path dependent" means.

Green's Theorem (Q15–Q16)

Green's theorem isn't on the Unit 6 slides — see Part 9 of the notes. It converts a line integral around a closed loop into a double integral over the region inside.

Q15. Green's Theorem — Rectangle Green's Theorem

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\oint_C (3x^2 - 2y)\,dx + (4x + y^2)\,dy\), where \(C\) is the positively oriented boundary of \(0 \le x \le 2\), \(0 \le y \le 1\).
Formula you need For a closed curve \(C\) traversed counterclockwise around region \(R\):
$$ \oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_R \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA $$
The idea — why Green's theorem is true \(Q_x - P_y\) is the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the curl: it measures how much the field spins around each tiny point. Chop the region into tiny squares and add up the circulation around each one. Where two squares share an edge, you travel that edge once in each direction, so those contributions cancel. The only edges that survive are on the outer boundary. So: total spin inside = circulation around the edge. That's the whole theorem.
Step 1 — Identify P and Q

\(P\) is whatever multiplies \(dx\); \(Q\) is whatever multiplies \(dy\).

$$ P = 3x^2 - 2y $$
$$ Q = 4x + y^2 $$
Step 2 — The integrand
$$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 4 - (-2) = 6 $$

Constant! The field spins by the same amount at every point.

Step 3 — Integrate over the rectangle

A constant integrand means "constant × area" — no real integration needed. The rectangle is 2 wide and 1 tall.

$$ \oint_C = \iint_R 6\,dA = 6 \times (2 \times 1) = 12 $$
\(12\)
What it means Going once counterclockwise around the rectangle, the field's total circulation is 12. Without Green's theorem you'd have to compute four separate line integrals (one per side) and add them — I did exactly that to check, and it also gives 12.

Q16. Green's Theorem — Triangle Green's Theorem

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\oint_C (y^2 + 2x)\,dx + (x^2 + 3y)\,dy\), where \(C\) is the positively oriented boundary of the triangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((2,0)\), \((2,1)\).
Formula you need
$$ \oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_R \left(Q_x - P_y\right) dA $$
plus a double integral over a non-rectangular region (Unit 3): inner limits may depend on the outer variable.
The idea Same theorem, but now the integrand isn't constant, so we must actually integrate — and the region is a triangle, so the hard part is describing the region with limits. Sketch it first.
Step 1 — The integrand
$$ P = y^2 + 2x, \quad Q = x^2 + 3y $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\quad Q_x - P_y = 2x - 2y $$
Step 2 — Describe the triangle

The three sides are: the bottom \(y = 0\) (from \((0,0)\) to \((2,0)\)); the right side \(x = 2\) (up to \((2,1)\)); and the slanted side from \((2,1)\) back to \((0,0)\). That slanted line passes through the origin with slope \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), so it's \(y = \tfrac{x}{2}\).

Slice the triangle into vertical strips. For each \(x\) between 0 and 2, a strip runs from the bottom \(y = 0\) up to the slanted side \(y = x/2\):

$$ 0 \le x \le 2 $$
$$ 0 \le y \le \frac{x}{2} $$
Step 3 — Inner integral (in y, with x held constant)
$$ \int_0^{x/2} (2x - 2y)\,dy = \Big[2xy - y^2\Big]_0^{x/2} $$
$$ = x^2 - \frac{x^2}{4} = \frac34 x^2 $$
Step 4 — Outer integral (in x)
$$ \int_0^2 \frac34 x^2\,dx = \frac34 \cdot \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \frac34 \cdot \frac83 = 2 $$
\(2\)
What it means The circulation counterclockwise around the triangle is 2. Checked independently by computing the three edge line integrals directly — they also total 2. The most common error here is the slanted side: if you write \(y = 2x\) instead of \(y = x/2\), you've described a different triangle.
