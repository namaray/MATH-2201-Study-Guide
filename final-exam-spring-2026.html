Q1. A Conservative Field; Area by Green's Theorem Units 6–7

(a) Consider the vector field
$$ \mathbf{F}(x,y) = (2xy + y^2)\,\mathbf{i} + (x^2 + 2xy)\,\mathbf{j}. $$
  1. Show that \(\mathbf{F}\) is a conservative vector field on the entire \(xy\)-plane.
  2. Find the potential function \(\phi(x, y)\).
  3. Find \(\displaystyle\int_{(0,0)}^{(1,2)} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\).
(b) Using Green's Theorem, find the area enclosed by the curve \(C:\ x = 2\cos t,\ y = 3\sin t,\ 0 \le t \le 2\pi\).

(a)(i) Show that F is conservative

Formula you need \(\mathbf{F} = P\,\mathbf{i} + Q\,\mathbf{j}\) is conservative on the whole plane exactly when
$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} $$
The idea "Conservative" means \(\mathbf{F}\) is the gradient of some function \(\phi\) — a "height" whose slope is \(\mathbf{F}\). If that's true, the mixed partials of \(\phi\) must agree (\(\phi_{xy} = \phi_{yx}\)), which is exactly the test \(P_y = Q_x\). (Background: Unit 6 notes.)
Step 1 — Read off \(P\) and \(Q\)

\(P\) is what multiplies \(\mathbf{i}\), \(Q\) is what multiplies \(\mathbf{j}\):

$$ P = 2xy + y^2, \qquad Q = x^2 + 2xy $$
Step 2 — Differentiate \(P\) with respect to \(y\)

Treat \(x\) as a constant. \(2xy \to 2x\) and \(y^2 \to 2y\):

$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 2x + 2y $$
Step 3 — Differentiate \(Q\) with respect to \(x\)

Treat \(y\) as a constant. \(x^2 \to 2x\) and \(2xy \to 2y\):

$$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 2x + 2y $$
Step 4 — Compare
$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 2x + 2y = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} $$

They are equal everywhere, and \(P\), \(Q\) are polynomials, so they are defined with continuous derivatives on the whole plane (no holes).

\(P_y = Q_x\) on the entire \(xy\)-plane, so \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative.

(a)(ii) Find the potential function

Formula you need We want \(\phi\) with
$$ \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = P, \qquad \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = Q $$
Step 1 — Integrate \(P\) with respect to \(x\)

Since \(\phi_x = P\), undo the \(x\)-derivative. \(y\) is a constant here:

$$ \phi = \int (2xy + y^2)\,dx $$
$$ = x^2y + xy^2 + g(y) $$

Why \(g(y)\) instead of \(+C\)? Anything that depends only on \(y\) disappears when you differentiate by \(x\), so the "constant" of integration can be any function of \(y\).

Step 2 — Differentiate that with respect to \(y\)
$$ \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = x^2 + 2xy + g'(y) $$
Step 3 — Set it equal to \(Q\) and solve for \(g\)
$$ x^2 + 2xy + g'(y) = x^2 + 2xy $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g'(y) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; g(y) = C $$
Step 4 — Put it together, and check
$$ \phi(x, y) = x^2y + xy^2 + C $$

Check: \(\phi_x = 2xy + y^2 = P\) ✓ and \(\phi_y = x^2 + 2xy = Q\) ✓.

\(\phi(x, y) = x^2y + xy^2 + C\)

(a)(iii) The line integral from (0, 0) to (1, 2)

Formula you need — the Fundamental Theorem for line integrals
$$ \int_A^B \nabla\phi \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(B) - \phi(A) $$
The idea For a conservative field, the work done only depends on where you start and where you finish — like the change in height on a hike. No path is given, and none is needed.
Step 1 — Value of \(\phi\) at the end point \((1, 2)\)
$$ \phi(1, 2) = (1)^2(2) + (1)(2)^2 = 2 + 4 = 6 $$
Step 2 — Value of \(\phi\) at the start point \((0, 0)\)
$$ \phi(0, 0) = 0 + 0 = 0 $$
Step 3 — Subtract (end minus start)
$$ \int_{(0,0)}^{(1,2)} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 6 - 0 = 6 $$

The \(+C\) cancels in the subtraction, which is why we can ignore it.

\(\displaystyle\int_{(0,0)}^{(1,2)} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 6\)
Check it the long way Take the straight line \(x = t\), \(y = 2t\), \(0 \le t \le 1\), so \(dx = dt\), \(dy = 2\,dt\). Then \(P = 2t(2t) + 4t^2 = 8t^2\) and \(Q = t^2 + 4t^2 = 5t^2\):
$$ \int_0^1 \left(8t^2 \cdot 1 + 5t^2 \cdot 2\right) dt = \int_0^1 18t^2\,dt $$
$$ = \Big[6t^3\Big]_0^1 = 6 \;✓ $$

(b) Area inside the curve, by Green's Theorem

Formula you need Green's Theorem: \(\displaystyle\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_R \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA\). With \(P = -\tfrac{y}{2}\), \(Q = \tfrac{x}{2}\) the bracket is \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1\), so
$$ \text{Area} = \iint_R 1\,dA = \frac12 \oint_C \left(x\,dy - y\,dx\right) $$
The idea Green's Theorem trades a double integral over the inside for a line integral around the edge. Choose \(P\), \(Q\) so that the inside integrand is just 1 — then the double integral is the area, and we only have to walk around the boundary once. (Background: Unit 7 notes.)
Step 1 — Write down \(x\), \(y\), \(dx\), \(dy\)
$$ x = 2\cos t, \qquad y = 3\sin t $$

Differentiate each with respect to \(t\), then multiply by \(dt\):

$$ dx = -2\sin t\,dt, \qquad dy = 3\cos t\,dt $$
Step 2 — Build \(x\,dy\) and \(y\,dx\) separately
$$ x\,dy = (2\cos t)(3\cos t\,dt) = 6\cos^2 t\,dt $$
$$ y\,dx = (3\sin t)(-2\sin t\,dt) = -6\sin^2 t\,dt $$
Step 3 — Subtract: \(x\,dy - y\,dx\)
$$ x\,dy - y\,dx = 6\cos^2 t\,dt - (-6\sin^2 t\,dt) $$
$$ = 6\left(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t\right) dt = 6\,dt $$

The two minus signs make a plus, and \(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\) — that's why this form is so convenient.

Step 4 — Integrate once round: \(t\) from 0 to \(2\pi\)
$$ \text{Area} = \frac12 \int_0^{2\pi} 6\,dt = \frac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 2\pi = 6\pi $$
Area \(= 6\pi \approx 18.85\)
What it means — and a check The area of an ellipse with semi-axes \(a\) and \(b\) is \(\pi ab\). Here \(a = 2\), \(b = 3\): \(\pi \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 6\pi\) ✓. (Using \(\oint x\,dy = \int_0^{2\pi} 6\cos^2 t\,dt = 6\pi\) also works — every version of the area formula gives the same answer.)

Q2. An Ellipse; Rotation of Axes; Gradient Conics · Unit 6

(a) Identify the conic section represented by the equation
$$ 16x^2 + 25y^2 - 64x + 50y - 311 = 0. $$
Hence, sketch its graph showing the center, vertices and foci clearly.
(b) Find the angle of rotation required to eliminate the \(xy\)-term in the equation
$$ 3x^2 + 8xy + 3y^2 - 10x + 5y - 7 = 0. $$
(c) Find a unit vector in the direction of maximum increase of \(f(x, y) = 4x^3y^2\) at the point \(P(-1, 1)\).

(a) Identify and sketch the conic

Formula you need Standard form of an ellipse with centre \((h, k)\) and a horizontal major axis (\(a > b\)):
$$ \frac{(x - h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y - k)^2}{b^2} = 1 $$
$$ c^2 = a^2 - b^2 $$
Vertices \((h \pm a, k)\), co-vertices \((h, k \pm b)\), foci \((h \pm c, k)\).
The idea There is no \(xy\)-term, so the conic isn't tilted — it's just shifted. Completing the square in \(x\) and in \(y\) reveals the shift (the centre) and the sizes \(a\), \(b\). (Background: Unit 4 notes.)
Step 1 — Group the \(x\) terms and the \(y\) terms; move the number across
$$ (16x^2 - 64x) + (25y^2 + 50y) = 311 $$
Step 2 — Factor out the coefficient of each square
$$ 16(x^2 - 4x) + 25(y^2 + 2y) = 311 $$
Step 3 — Complete each square

Half of \(-4\) is \(-2\), and \((-2)^2 = 4\). Half of \(2\) is \(1\), and \(1^2 = 1\):

$$ x^2 - 4x = (x - 2)^2 - 4 $$
$$ y^2 + 2y = (y + 1)^2 - 1 $$
Step 4 — Substitute back and tidy up
$$ 16\big[(x-2)^2 - 4\big] + 25\big[(y+1)^2 - 1\big] = 311 $$
$$ 16(x-2)^2 - 64 + 25(y+1)^2 - 25 = 311 $$
$$ 16(x-2)^2 + 25(y+1)^2 = 311 + 64 + 25 $$
$$ 16(x-2)^2 + 25(y+1)^2 = 400 $$
Step 5 — Divide by 400 to get a 1 on the right
$$ \frac{16(x-2)^2}{400} + \frac{25(y+1)^2}{400} = 1 $$
$$ \frac{(x-2)^2}{25} + \frac{(y+1)^2}{16} = 1 $$
Step 6 — Identify it

Two squared terms, both plus, with different denominators → an ellipse. (Quick test from the original equation: \(A = 16\), \(C = 25\) have the same sign, so \(B^2 - 4AC = -1600 < 0\) → ellipse.)

Step 7 — Read off \(h, k, a, b\), then find \(c\)

Compare with \(\frac{(x-h)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(y-k)^2}{b^2} = 1\). Watch the signs: \(y + 1 = y - (-1)\), so \(k = -1\).

$$ h = 2, \qquad k = -1 $$
$$ a^2 = 25 \Rightarrow a = 5, \qquad b^2 = 16 \Rightarrow b = 4 $$

The bigger number (25) is under \(x\), so the long axis is horizontal.

$$ c^2 = a^2 - b^2 = 25 - 16 = 9 \;\Longrightarrow\; c = 3 $$
Step 8 — The key points
  • Centre \((h, k) = (2, -1)\)
  • Vertices — go \(a = 5\) left and right: \((2 \pm 5, -1) = (7, -1)\) and \((-3, -1)\)
  • Co-vertices — go \(b = 4\) up and down: \((2, -1 \pm 4) = (2, 3)\) and \((2, -5)\)
  • Foci — go \(c = 3\) left and right: \((2 \pm 3, -1) = (5, -1)\) and \((-1, -1)\)
Step 9 — Sketch
An ellipse \(\dfrac{(x-2)^2}{25} + \dfrac{(y+1)^2}{16} = 1\): centre \((2,-1)\), vertices \((7,-1)\), \((-3,-1)\), foci \((5,-1)\), \((-1,-1)\).
Extra facts (often asked) Major axis length \(2a = 10\), minor axis length \(2b = 8\), eccentricity \(e = c/a = 3/5\). Check a vertex in the original equation, at \((7, -1)\):
$$ 16(49) + 25(1) - 64(7) + 50(-1) - 311 $$
$$ = 784 + 25 - 448 - 50 - 311 = 0 \;✓ $$

(b) Angle that removes the xy-term

Formula you need For \(Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0\), rotate the axes by the angle \(\theta\) with
$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B} $$
The idea An \(xy\)-term means the conic is tilted. Turning the coordinate axes by the right angle lines them up with the conic, and the \(xy\)-term disappears. (More on this: rotation of axes explained.)
Step 1 — Read off \(A\), \(B\), \(C\)

\(A\) goes with \(x^2\), \(B\) with \(xy\), \(C\) with \(y^2\):

$$ A = 3, \qquad B = 8, \qquad C = 3 $$
Step 2 — Put them in the formula
$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B} = \frac{3 - 3}{8} = \frac{0}{8} = 0 $$
Step 3 — Solve for \(\theta\)

\(\cot\) is \(\cos/\sin\), and it is 0 when \(\cos = 0\). For \(0 < 2\theta < \pi\) that happens at \(2\theta = \pi/2\):

$$ 2\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \theta = \frac{\pi}{4} $$
\(\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4} = 45^\circ\)
Check — the xy-term really disappears For \(\theta = 45^\circ\): \(x = \frac{x' - y'}{\sqrt2}\), \(y = \frac{x' + y'}{\sqrt2}\). Then
$$ 3x^2 + 3y^2 = 3(x'^2 + y'^2) $$
$$ 8xy = 8\cdot\frac{x'^2 - y'^2}{2} = 4x'^2 - 4y'^2 $$
Adding: \(7x'^2 - y'^2\) — no \(x'y'\) term ✓. One positive and one negative square means the curve is a hyperbola (also \(B^2 - 4AC = 64 - 36 = 28 > 0\)).

(c) Direction of maximum increase

Formula you need \(f\) increases fastest in the direction of its gradient:
$$ \nabla f = f_x\,\mathbf{i} + f_y\,\mathbf{j}, \qquad \mathbf{u} = \frac{\nabla f}{\|\nabla f\|} $$
The idea The gradient is an arrow pointing "most uphill". The question wants a unit vector, so after finding the gradient we divide it by its own length.
Step 1 — Partial derivative with respect to \(x\)

\(y^2\) is a constant; \(4x^3 \to 12x^2\):

$$ f_x = 12x^2y^2 $$
Step 2 — Partial derivative with respect to \(y\)

\(4x^3\) is a constant; \(y^2 \to 2y\):

$$ f_y = 4x^3 \cdot 2y = 8x^3y $$
Step 3 — Evaluate at \(P(-1, 1)\)
$$ f_x(-1, 1) = 12(-1)^2(1)^2 = 12 $$
$$ f_y(-1, 1) = 8(-1)^3(1) = -8 $$
$$ \nabla f(-1, 1) = 12\,\mathbf{i} - 8\,\mathbf{j} $$
Step 4 — Its length
$$ \|\nabla f\| = \sqrt{12^2 + (-8)^2} = \sqrt{144 + 64} = \sqrt{208} $$
$$ \sqrt{208} = \sqrt{16 \cdot 13} = 4\sqrt{13} $$
Step 5 — Divide to make it a unit vector
$$ \mathbf{u} = \frac{12\,\mathbf{i} - 8\,\mathbf{j}}{4\sqrt{13}} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{13}}\,\mathbf{i} - \frac{2}{\sqrt{13}}\,\mathbf{j} $$
\(\mathbf{u} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{13}}\left(3\,\mathbf{i} - 2\,\mathbf{j}\right)\)
What it means Walking from \(P\) in the direction \(\mathbf{u}\), \(f\) rises at its fastest possible rate, \(\|\nabla f\| = 4\sqrt{13} \approx 14.4\) per unit. Check that \(\mathbf{u}\) is a unit vector: \(\frac{9}{13} + \frac{4}{13} = 1\) ✓.

Q3. A Triple Integral; a Line Integral; a Space Curve Coordinates · Unit 6

(a) Use appropriate coordinates to evaluate
$$ \int_{-3}^{3}\int_{-\sqrt{9-x^2}}^{\sqrt{9-x^2}}\int_0^{\sqrt{9-x^2-y^2}} f\;dz\,dy\,dx, $$
$$ \text{where } f = x^2 + y^2 + z^2. $$
(b) Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_C \left(x^2y\,dx + x\,dy\right)\), where \(C\) consists of the line segments from \((0,0) \to (2,0) \to (2,3)\).
(c) Sketch the parametric curve represented by the equation \(x = 2\cos t,\ y = 2\sin t,\ z = 1\).

(a) Read the region, then switch to spherical coordinates

Formula you need — spherical coordinates
$$ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \rho^2 $$
$$ dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
The idea Work out what solid the limits describe. If it's round, and the integrand is \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\), spherical coordinates turn a horrible Cartesian integral into three easy ones. (Background: Unit 5 notes.)
Step 1 — Read the limits from the outside in
  • \(x\): \(-3\) to \(3\).
  • \(y\): \(-\sqrt{9-x^2}\) to \(\sqrt{9-x^2}\). Squaring \(y = \pm\sqrt{9 - x^2}\) gives \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\): the bottom and top halves of a circle of radius 3. Together with the \(x\) limits, the shadow on the \(xy\)-plane is the full disc \(x^2 + y^2 \le 9\).
  • \(z\): \(0\) to \(\sqrt{9 - x^2 - y^2}\). The floor is \(z = 0\). Squaring the roof gives \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9\) with \(z \ge 0\): the top half of a sphere of radius 3.

So the solid is the upper half-ball of radius 3.

Step 2 — The spherical limits
  • \(\rho\) (distance from the origin): from the centre \(0\) out to the dome \(3\).
  • \(\phi\) (angle down from the \(+z\)-axis): from \(0\) (straight up) to \(\pi/2\) (the flat floor). Going past \(\pi/2\) would go below \(z = 0\).
  • \(\theta\) (angle around): the full turn \(0\) to \(2\pi\), because the shadow is a whole disc.
Step 3 — Convert the integrand and \(dV\)
$$ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \rho^2 $$
$$ \left(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\right) dV = \rho^2 \cdot \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
$$ = \rho^4\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
Step 4 — Write the new integral
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{3} \rho^4\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
Step 5 — The limits are all constants, so split it into three integrals
$$ = \left(\int_0^{3}\rho^4\,d\rho\right)\left(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\phi\,d\phi\right)\left(\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\right) $$
Step 6 — Do each one
$$ \int_0^{3}\rho^4\,d\rho = \left[\frac{\rho^5}{5}\right]_0^3 = \frac{243}{5} $$
$$ \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\phi\,d\phi = \Big[-\cos\phi\Big]_0^{\pi/2} $$
$$ = -0 - (-1) = 1 $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}d\theta = 2\pi $$
Step 7 — Multiply
$$ \frac{243}{5} \cdot 1 \cdot 2\pi = \frac{486\pi}{5} $$
\(\dfrac{486\pi}{5} \approx 305.4\)

(b) Line integral along two segments

Formula you need Split the path into pieces, parametrise each, and add:
$$ \int_C = \int_{C_1} + \int_{C_2} $$
The idea Here \(P = x^2y\) and \(Q = x\), so \(P_y = x^2\) but \(Q_x = 1\): the field is not conservative, and there's no shortcut — we must follow the actual path. Each segment is horizontal or vertical, so on each one, one of \(dx\), \(dy\) is zero.
Step 1 — Segment \(C_1\): \((0,0) \to (2,0)\)

Along the \(x\)-axis, \(y = 0\) the whole time, so \(dy = 0\). Parametrise: \(x = t\), \(y = 0\), \(0 \le t \le 2\).

$$ x^2y\,dx = t^2 \cdot 0 \cdot dt = 0, \qquad x\,dy = t \cdot 0 = 0 $$
$$ \int_{C_1} = \int_0^2 0\,dt = 0 $$
Step 2 — Segment \(C_2\): \((2,0) \to (2,3)\)

Straight up the line \(x = 2\), so \(dx = 0\). Parametrise: \(x = 2\), \(y = t\), \(0 \le t \le 3\), so \(dy = dt\).

$$ x^2y\,dx = (4)(t)(0) = 0, \qquad x\,dy = 2\,dt $$
$$ \int_{C_2} = \int_0^3 2\,dt = \Big[2t\Big]_0^3 = 6 $$
Step 3 — Add the pieces
$$ \int_C = 0 + 6 = 6 $$
\(\displaystyle\int_C \left(x^2y\,dx + x\,dy\right) = 6\)
About the reading. On the photographed paper the integrand is partly covered by handwriting; it's read here as \(x^2y\,dx + x\,dy\). The method is the same whatever the exact integrand: on \(C_1\) only the \(dx\) part survives (with \(y = 0\)), and on \(C_2\) only the \(dy\) part survives (with \(x = 2\)). For example, if it were \(xy\,dy\), Step 2 would give \(\int_0^3 2t\,dt = 9\).

(c) Sketch x = 2 cos t, y = 2 sin t, z = 1

The idea Look at the pieces separately: \(x\) and \(y\) together describe a circle, and \(z\) never changes. So it's a circle, lifted to height 1.
Step 1 — Eliminate \(t\) from \(x\) and \(y\)
$$ x^2 + y^2 = 4\cos^2 t + 4\sin^2 t $$
$$ = 4\left(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t\right) = 4 $$

So the point is always distance 2 from the \(z\)-axis: a circle of radius 2.

Step 2 — Look at \(z\)

\(z = 1\) for every \(t\), so the curve lies flat in the horizontal plane \(z = 1\), one unit above the \(xy\)-plane.

Step 3 — Plot a few points to get the direction
\(t\)\(0\)\(\pi/2\)\(\pi\)\(3\pi/2\)\(2\pi\)
\((x, y, z)\)\((2, 0, 1)\)\((0, 2, 1)\)\((-2, 0, 1)\)\((0, -2, 1)\)\((2, 0, 1)\)

\(x\) to \(y\) to \(-x\) to \(-y\): it goes counterclockwise when seen from above, and returns to the start at \(t = 2\pi\).

Step 4 — Sketch
A circle of radius 2, centre \((0, 0, 1)\), in the plane \(z = 1\), traced counterclockwise (seen from above).

Q4. Surface Integral over a Cone; Divergence Theorem Unit 7

(a) Evaluate the surface integral \(\displaystyle\iint_\sigma x^2z\,dS\), where \(\sigma\) is the part of the cone \(z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) that lies between the planes \(z = 1\) and \(z = 2\).
(b) Use the Divergence Theorem to find the outward flux of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = x\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\) across the surface of the region enclosed by the hemisphere \(z = \sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2}\) and the plane \(z = 0\).

(a) Surface integral over part of a cone

Formula you need For a surface \(z = g(x, y)\) over its shadow \(R\):
$$ \iint_\sigma f\,dS $$
$$ = \iint_R f\big(x, y, g(x,y)\big)\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\;dA $$
The idea A tilted patch of surface \(dS\) is bigger than its flat shadow \(dA\). The square root is the stretch factor. So: flatten the problem onto the shadow, multiply by the stretch, and integrate there. (Background: Unit 7 notes.)
Step 1 — The surface: \(g\) and its partial derivatives
$$ g = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \left(x^2 + y^2\right)^{1/2} $$

Chain rule: bring down the \(\tfrac12\), lower the power, multiply by the derivative of the inside (\(2x\) or \(2y\)):

$$ g_x = \frac12\left(x^2 + y^2\right)^{-1/2}(2x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} $$
$$ g_y = \frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}} $$
Step 2 — The stretch factor
$$ g_x^2 + g_y^2 = \frac{x^2}{x^2 + y^2} + \frac{y^2}{x^2 + y^2} = \frac{x^2 + y^2}{x^2 + y^2} = 1 $$
$$ \sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt2 $$

A constant! The cone has the same slope (45°) everywhere, so every patch is stretched by the same factor \(\sqrt2\): \(dS = \sqrt2\,dA\).

Step 3 — The shadow \(R\)

On the cone, \(z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = r\). So "\(z\) between 1 and 2" means "\(r\) between 1 and 2":

$$ R:\quad 1 \le r \le 2, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi $$

A ring (annulus) — so use polar coordinates, with \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\).

Step 4 — The integrand on the surface, in polar

Replace \(z\) by \(g = r\), and \(x\) by \(r\cos\theta\):

$$ x^2z = \left(r\cos\theta\right)^2 \cdot r = r^3\cos^2\theta $$
Step 5 — Set up the integral
$$ \iint_\sigma x^2z\,dS = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_1^2 r^3\cos^2\theta \cdot \sqrt2 \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = \sqrt2\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_1^2 r^4\cos^2\theta\,dr\,d\theta $$

Three factors multiply: the integrand \(r^3\cos^2\theta\), the stretch \(\sqrt2\), and the polar \(r\) from \(dA\).

Step 6 — The \(r\)-integral
$$ \int_1^2 r^4\,dr = \left[\frac{r^5}{5}\right]_1^2 = \frac{32}{5} - \frac{1}{5} = \frac{31}{5} $$
Step 7 — The \(\theta\)-integral
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}\,d\theta $$
$$ = \left[\frac{\theta}{2} + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\right]_0^{2\pi} = \pi $$
Step 8 — Multiply everything
$$ \sqrt2 \cdot \frac{31}{5} \cdot \pi = \frac{31\sqrt2\,\pi}{5} $$
\(\displaystyle\iint_\sigma x^2z\,dS = \frac{31\sqrt2\,\pi}{5} \approx 27.55\)

(b) Outward flux by the Divergence Theorem

Formula you need
$$ \oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_G \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\;dV $$
$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z} $$
The idea The dome plus the flat floor make a closed surface. Instead of two surface integrals, add up the divergence (the "outflow per unit volume") over the solid inside.
Step 1 — Identify the solid

Squaring \(z = \sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2}\) gives \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\) with \(z \ge 0\): the top half of a sphere of radius 2. With the floor \(z = 0\), the solid \(G\) is the upper half-ball of radius 2.

Step 2 — The divergence

\(P = x\), \(Q = y\), \(R = z\):

$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial z} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 $$
Step 3 — The triple integral is "3 × volume"
$$ \iiint_G 3\,dV = 3 \times \text{Volume}(G) $$
Step 4 — Volume of the half-ball

A whole ball has volume \(\tfrac43\pi a^3\); half of it is \(\tfrac23\pi a^3\). With \(a = 2\):

$$ \text{Volume}(G) = \frac23\pi(2)^3 = \frac{16\pi}{3} $$
Step 5 — Multiply
$$ \text{Flux} = 3 \cdot \frac{16\pi}{3} = 16\pi $$
Outward flux \(= 16\pi \approx 50.27\)
Two checks Spherical coordinates:
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^2 3\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
$$ = 3 \cdot \frac{8}{3} \cdot 1 \cdot 2\pi = 16\pi \;✓ $$
Directly: on the dome, \(\mathbf{F} = \langle x, y, z\rangle\) points straight out, with \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = \rho = 2\); the dome's area is \(2\pi a^2 = 8\pi\), giving \(2 \cdot 8\pi = 16\pi\). On the floor \(\mathbf{n} = -\mathbf{k}\) and \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = -z = 0\), giving 0. Total \(16\pi\) ✓.
