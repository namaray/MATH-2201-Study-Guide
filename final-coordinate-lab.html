1. The polar patch — why \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\)

A small step \(\Delta r\) outwards and a small turn \(\Delta\theta\) cut out a curved patch. Watch how its size depends on \(r\).

Orange patch: \(r\) to \(r + \Delta r\), \(\theta\) to \(\theta + \Delta\theta\)

Radial side (straight)
Arc side \(= r\,\Delta\theta\) (θ in radians)
True area of the patch
\(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\) (with the Jacobian)
\(\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\) (forgetting it)
Stretch factor: true area ÷ \((\Delta r\,\Delta\theta)\)
The idea The patch is almost a rectangle: one side is \(\Delta r\) long, the other is an arc of length \(r\,\Delta\theta\) (arc = radius × angle). So its area is about $$ \Delta r \times r\,\Delta\theta = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta. $$ The extra \(r\) is the Jacobian: the same turn \(\Delta\theta\) sweeps a longer arc further out.
Try this
  • Tick the box: same \(\Delta r\) and \(\Delta\theta\), yet the outer patches are much bigger.
  • Drag \(r\) from 1 to 4: the stretch factor follows \(r\).
  • Make \(\Delta r\) and \(\Delta\theta\) tiny: \(r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\) becomes exact.

2. The Jacobian as stretching

A change of variables bends a grid of identical little squares in the \((u, v)\)-plane into a grid of curved patches in the \((x, y)\)-plane. \(|J|\) is how much each square's area gets multiplied.

Tap or hover a square on the left to find where it lands.

Chosen square
Its area \(\Delta u\,\Delta v\)
Area of its image
Stretch: image ÷ square
\(|J|\) at the square's centre
The idea That's why \(dx\,dy = |J|\,du\,dv\): every tiny square of area \(du\,dv\) turns into a patch of area \(|J|\,du\,dv\).
Try this
  • Polar: squares in the same row (same \(r\)) all stretch by the same amount; outer rows stretch more.
  • Linear: every square stretches by the same amount — a constant Jacobian.
  • \(uv\) example (from the slides): the stretch changes from square to square, and still matches \(2u/v\).

The idea
  • \(\theta\) limits: the first and last angles where the ray touches the region.
  • \(r\) limits: inner edge → outer edge, written as functions of \(\theta\).
  • The circle \(r = 4\cos\theta\) is traced once for \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\). Going to \(2\pi\) would cover it twice.

4. 3-D point explorer — cylindrical & spherical

Move a point with either set of coordinates and watch the other two descriptions update. Drag the picture to rotate it.

Drag to rotate.

Cartesian \((x, y, z)\)
Cylindrical \((r, \theta, z)\)
Spherical \((\rho, \theta, \phi)\)
The idea
  • Both systems share the same \(\theta\): the angle of the point's shadow on the \(xy\)-plane.
  • \(r\) = distance from the \(z\)-axis; \(\rho\) = distance from the origin. They form a right triangle with \(z\): \(r = \rho\sin\phi\), \(z = \rho\cos\phi\).
  • \(\phi\) is measured down from the \(+z\)-axis: \(0\) at the top, \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) on the \(xy\)-plane, \(\pi\) at the bottom.
Try this
  • Cylindrical: keep \(r\) fixed and move \(\theta\) and \(z\) — the point slides around a cylinder.
  • Spherical: keep \(\rho\) fixed — it moves on a sphere. Keep \(\phi\) fixed — it moves on a cone.
  • Set \(\phi = 90^\circ\): the point drops onto the \(xy\)-plane, and \(\rho = r\).

5. 3-D volume element — where \(r\) and \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) come from

Small steps in each coordinate cut out a curved box. Its three edges give its volume — and the Jacobian.

Drag to rotate. The three labelled edges are the box's dimensions.

True volume of the box
The idea The box's edges are \(\Delta r\) (out), \(r\,\Delta\theta\) (around) and \(\Delta z\) (up). Multiply them: $$ dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz. $$ It's the polar patch from simulation 1, stacked \(\Delta z\) high.
The idea The box's edges are \(\Delta\rho\) (out), \(\rho\,\Delta\phi\) (down an arc of radius \(\rho\)) and \(\rho\sin\phi\,\Delta\theta\) (around a circle of radius \(r = \rho\sin\phi\)). Multiply them: $$ dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta. $$ Near the poles (\(\phi\) close to 0) the box gets squashed thin — that's the \(\sin\phi\).
Try this
  • Spherical: move \(\phi\) towards \(5^\circ\) and watch the "around" edge shrink.
  • Shrink every \(\Delta\): the Jacobian formula and the true volume agree.
