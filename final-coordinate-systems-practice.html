Polar Coordinates (Q1–Q6)

Q1. Cartesian → Polar Point

Convert the Cartesian point \((3, 4)\) to polar coordinates.
$$ r = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5 $$

\(x > 0\), \(y > 0\) → Quadrant I, so \(\tan^{-1}\) gives the angle directly:

$$ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac43 \approx 0.927 \text{ rad} \approx 53.13^\circ $$
\(\left(5,\ \tan^{-1}\tfrac43\right) \approx (5,\ 0.927)\)

Q2. Cartesian → Polar PointAssignment

Convert the Cartesian point \((-5, 12)\) to polar coordinates.
Show solution
$$ r = \sqrt{(-5)^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13 $$

\(x < 0\), \(y > 0\) → Quadrant II. Reference angle:

$$ \alpha = \tan^{-1}\frac{12}{5} \approx 1.176 \text{ rad} \approx 67.38^\circ $$
$$ \theta = \pi - \alpha \approx 1.966 \text{ rad} \approx 112.62^\circ $$
\(\left(13,\ \pi - \tan^{-1}\tfrac{12}{5}\right) \approx (13,\ 1.966)\)
This is the question the quadrant rule exists for. A calculator's \(\tan^{-1}(12/{-5})\) gives \(-67.38^\circ\), which points into Quadrant IV — the wrong side of the plane.

Q3. Polar → Cartesian PointAssignment

Convert the polar point \(\left(5, \tfrac{\pi}{6}\right)\) to Cartesian coordinates.
Show solution
$$ x = 5\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = 5\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac{5\sqrt3}{2} $$
$$ y = 5\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = 5\cdot\frac12 = \frac52 $$
\(\left(\tfrac{5\sqrt3}{2},\ \tfrac52\right) \approx (4.330,\ 2.5)\)

Q4. Polar → Cartesian Point

Convert \(\left(6, \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\right)\) from polar to Cartesian coordinates.

\(\tfrac{4\pi}{3} = 240^\circ\), in Quadrant III — expect both coordinates negative.

$$ x = 6\cos\frac{4\pi}{3} = 6\left(-\frac12\right) = -3 $$
$$ y = 6\sin\frac{4\pi}{3} = 6\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = -3\sqrt3 $$
\(\left(-3,\ -3\sqrt3\right) \approx (-3,\ -5.196)\)

Q5. Cartesian Equation → Polar Equation

Convert \(x^2 + y^2 = 6x\) to a polar equation.

Substitute \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) and \(x = r\cos\theta\):

$$ r^2 = 6r\cos\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; r = 6\cos\theta $$
\(r = 6\cos\theta\)
Dividing by \(r\) looks like it throws away the solution \(r = 0\), but the origin is still on the curve: \(r = 6\cos\theta\) gives \(r = 0\) at \(\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\). Completing the square shows it's the circle \((x-3)^2 + y^2 = 9\) — centre \((3, 0)\), radius 3.

Q6. Area in Polar Double IntegralAssignment

Find the area inside the circle \(r = 4\cos\theta\).
Show solution

\(r \ge 0\) requires \(\cos\theta \ge 0\), so the circle is traced once for \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\).

$$ A = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{4\cos\theta} r\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \frac{16\cos^2\theta}{2}\,d\theta = 8\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta $$
$$ = 8\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}\,d\theta $$
$$ = 4\left[\theta + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{2}\right]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = 4\pi $$
\(A = 4\pi \approx 12.57\)
Check: \(r = 4\cos\theta\) is \(x^2 + y^2 = 4x\), a circle of radius 2, area \(\pi(2)^2 = 4\pi\). ✓ If you'd integrated \(\theta\) from 0 to \(2\pi\) you'd have got \(8\pi\) — the circle traced twice.

Cylindrical Coordinates (Q7–Q10)

Q7. Cartesian → Cylindrical PointAssignment

Convert the Cartesian point \((3, 4, 5)\) to cylindrical coordinates.
Show solution

\(r\) and \(\theta\) come from \((x, y) = (3, 4)\) exactly as in Q1; \(z\) is unchanged.

$$ r = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5 $$
$$ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac43 \approx 0.927 $$
$$ z = 5 $$
\(\left(5,\ \tan^{-1}\tfrac43,\ 5\right) \approx (5,\ 0.927,\ 5)\)

Q8. Cartesian Equation → Cylindrical Equation

Convert the Cartesian equation \(x^2 + y^2 = 16\) to cylindrical coordinates.
$$ x^2 + y^2 = r^2 = 16 \;\Longrightarrow\; r = 4 $$
\(r = 4\) — a cylinder of radius 4 around the \(z\)-axis
In 2D this equation is a circle; in 3D, with \(z\) free, it's a whole cylinder. That's where "cylindrical" coordinates get their name.

Q9. Cartesian Equation → Cylindrical EquationAssignment

Convert \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 25\) to cylindrical coordinates.
Show solution

Replace only \(x^2 + y^2\) with \(r^2\); \(z\) stays as it is.

$$ r^2 + z^2 = 25 $$
\(r^2 + z^2 = 25\) — a sphere of radius 5 (or \(z = \pm\sqrt{25 - r^2}\))
In spherical coordinates the same surface is simply \(\rho = 5\) — a hint that spherical is the natural system for spheres.

Q10. Volume of a Cylinder Triple Integral

Find the volume inside the cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\), between \(z = 0\) and \(z = 5\).

Limits: \(0 \le r \le 2\), \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\), \(0 \le z \le 5\). Don't forget the Jacobian \(r\).

$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^5 r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 5r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\left[\frac{5r^2}{2}\right]_0^2 d\theta $$
$$ = \int_0^{2\pi} 10\,d\theta = 20\pi $$
\(V = 20\pi \approx 62.83\) cubic units
Check: \(\pi r^2 h = \pi(2)^2(5) = 20\pi\). ✓

Spherical Coordinates (Q11–Q14)

Q11. Cartesian → Spherical Point

Convert the Cartesian point \((1, 1, 1)\) to spherical coordinates.
$$ \rho = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt3 $$
$$ \theta = \tan^{-1}\frac{1}{1} = \frac{\pi}{4} \quad (\text{Quadrant I}) $$
$$ \phi = \cos^{-1}\frac{z}{\rho} = \cos^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt3} $$
$$ \approx 0.955 \text{ rad} \approx 54.74^\circ $$
\((\rho, \theta, \phi) = \left(\sqrt3,\ \tfrac{\pi}{4},\ \cos^{-1}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right)\)
\(\approx (1.732,\ 0.785,\ 0.955)\)
Sanity check on \(\phi\): the point is above the \(xy\)-plane (\(z > 0\)), so \(\phi\) must be less than \(90^\circ\). ✓

Q12. Spherical → Cartesian Point

Convert \(\left(4, \tfrac{\pi}{3}, \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\) into Cartesian coordinates.
The prompt on the sheet is garbled — it contains two overlapping versions of the point, one of which is broken. This solution uses the cleanly typeset one, \(\left(4, \tfrac{\pi}{3}, \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\), read in the slides' order \((\rho, \theta, \phi)\): \(\rho = 4\), \(\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\), \(\phi = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\).
$$ x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta = 4\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac12 = \sqrt2 $$
$$ y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta = 4\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \sqrt6 $$
$$ z = \rho\cos\phi = 4\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2} = 2\sqrt2 $$
\(\left(\sqrt2,\ \sqrt6,\ 2\sqrt2\right)\)
\(\approx (1.414,\ 2.449,\ 2.828)\)
Check: \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 2 + 6 + 8 = 16 = \rho^2\). ✓
If the intended point was the other version, \(\left(4, \tfrac{\pi}{3}, \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)\), only \(z\) changes sign: \(\left(\sqrt2,\ \sqrt6,\ -2\sqrt2\right)\), because \(\cos\tfrac{3\pi}{4} = -\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\) while \(\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\) is unchanged.

Q13. Volume of a Ball Triple IntegralAssignment

Find the volume of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 9\) using spherical coordinates.
Show solution

Radius 3, whole ball: \(0 \le \rho \le 3\), \(0 \le \phi \le \pi\), \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\).

$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^{3} \rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$

Constant limits, so it splits into three single integrals:

$$ \int_0^3 \rho^2\,d\rho = 9 $$
$$ \int_0^{\pi}\sin\phi\,d\phi = 2 $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta = 2\pi $$
$$ V = 9 \cdot 2 \cdot 2\pi = 36\pi $$
\(V = 36\pi \approx 113.10\) cubic units
Check: \(\tfrac43\pi(3)^3 = 36\pi\). ✓

Q14. Volume of a Hemisphere Triple Integral

Find the volume inside the sphere \(\rho \le 4\) and above the \(xy\)-plane.

"Above the \(xy\)-plane" means \(z \ge 0\), which in spherical coordinates is \(0 \le \phi \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\). \(\theta\) still goes all the way round.

$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{4} \rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
$$ \int_0^4 \rho^2\,d\rho = \frac{64}{3} $$
$$ \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\phi\,d\phi = \big[-\cos\phi\big]_0^{\pi/2} = 1 $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta = 2\pi $$
$$ V = \frac{64}{3}\cdot 1\cdot 2\pi = \frac{128\pi}{3} $$
\(V = \dfrac{128\pi}{3} \approx 134.04\) cubic units
Check: half of \(\tfrac43\pi(4)^3 = \tfrac{256\pi}{3}\) is \(\tfrac{128\pi}{3}\). ✓ The only change from a full ball is the \(\phi\) limit.
