Q1 Explained — Rotating a Tilted Conic

Final Exam Review, Question 1 · every step, slowly, with pictures · for the short version see the Final Exam Review

The Big Picture

What is this question actually about?

You already know how to read a conic like \(\dfrac{x^2}{9} + \dfrac{y^2}{4} = 1\): it's an ellipse sitting straight, lined up with the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes.

The problem in Q1 is the \(xy\)-term (the \(24xy\)). An \(xy\)-term means the curve is tilted — turned at an angle — so none of the standard forms fit.

Think of a photo hanging crooked on a wall. You could straighten the photo — or just tilt your head. Rotation of axes is tilting your head: we draw a new pair of axes, \(X\) and \(Y\), turned by an angle \(\theta\) so they line up with the curve. Measured with those new axes the curve isn't tilted any more, the \(xy\)-term disappears, and we can recognise it.

The curve never moves. Only the axes (the "rulers" we measure with) turn.
The plan — exactly the six parts of the question
  1. Find the angle \(\theta\) to turn the axes by.
  2. Write how old coordinates \((x, y)\) and new ones \((X, Y)\) are related.
  3. Substitute, so the equation is written in \(X\) and \(Y\) (no \(xy\)-term left).
  4. Tidy it into a standard form (complete the square).
  5. Say which conic it is.
  6. Describe it: vertex, focus, and so on.

Step 0 — Read Off the Coefficients

Match it to the general form

Every conic can be written as

$$ {\color{#c0392b}A}x^2 + {\color{#8e44ad}B}xy + {\color{#1e8449}C}y^2 + {\color{#2471a3}D}x + {\color{#2471a3}E}y + F = 0 $$

Line ours up with it, term by term:

$$ {\color{#c0392b}9}x^2 + {\color{#8e44ad}24}xy + {\color{#1e8449}16}y^2 {\color{#2471a3}- 30}x {\color{#2471a3}- 40}y + 0 = 0 $$
A = 9B = 24C = 16 D = −30E = −40F = 0
⚠ \(B\) is the whole number in front of \(xy\) — here 24, not 12. And keep the minus signs on \(D\) and \(E\).

(a) Find the Angle \(\theta\)

The formula, and what it gives

The angle that kills the \(xy\)-term always satisfies

$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{{\color{#c0392b}A} - {\color{#1e8449}C}}{{\color{#8e44ad}B}} $$

(You'll see why it works in the slider below — at exactly this angle the \(xy\)-coefficient hits zero.) Plug in:

$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{{\color{#c0392b}9} - {\color{#1e8449}16}}{{\color{#8e44ad}24}} = -\frac{7}{24} $$

That's a value for \(\cot 2\theta\), but we want \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\). Two small moves get us there.

Move 1 — turn \(\cot 2\theta\) into a triangle

\(\cot = \dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{opposite}}\), so \(\cot 2\theta = \dfrac{-7}{24}\) means a right triangle with adjacent side \(-7\) and opposite side \(24\). Its hypotenuse is \(\sqrt{7^2 + 24^2} = \sqrt{49 + 576} = \sqrt{625} = 25\) (a 7-24-25 triangle — like 3-4-5).

$$ \cos 2\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} = -\frac{7}{25} $$
Negative \(\cos 2\theta\) just means \(2\theta\) is between \(90^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\). That's fine — \(\theta\) itself is then between \(45^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\).

Move 2 — the half-angle formulas

These turn \(\cos 2\theta\) into \(\cos\theta\) and \(\sin\theta\):

$$ \cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \frac{7}{25}}{2}} $$
$$ = \sqrt{\frac{18/25}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{25}} = \frac35 $$
$$ \sin\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \frac{7}{25}}{2}} $$
$$ = \sqrt{\frac{32/25}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{16}{25}} = \frac45 $$
⚠ Watch the signs inside: \(1 + \cos 2\theta = 1 + (-\tfrac{7}{25}) = 1 - \tfrac{7}{25}\). Both square roots are taken positive, because \(\theta\) is between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\).
(a) \(\cos\theta = \tfrac35\), \(\sin\theta = \tfrac45\), so \(\tan\theta = \tfrac43\) and \(\theta = \tan^{-1}\tfrac43 \approx 53.13^\circ\)

Quick check: \(\tan 2\theta = \dfrac{2\tan\theta}{1 - \tan^2\theta} = \dfrac{8/3}{1 - 16/9} = \dfrac{8/3}{-7/9} = -\dfrac{24}{7}\), which is \(1 \div \left(-\tfrac{7}{24}\right)\). ✓

(b) The Transformation Equations

One point, two addresses

Every point now has two sets of coordinates: its old address \((x, y)\), measured along the usual axes, and its new address \((X, Y)\), measured along the turned axes. The transformation equations convert one into the other.

The rule (memorise this pair)
$$ x = X\cos\theta - Y\sin\theta $$
$$ y = X\sin\theta + Y\cos\theta $$

Put in \(\cos\theta = \tfrac35\), \(\sin\theta = \tfrac45\):

$$ x = \tfrac35 X - \tfrac45 Y = \frac{3X - 4Y}{5} $$
$$ y = \tfrac45 X + \tfrac35 Y = \frac{4X + 3Y}{5} $$
(b) \(x = \dfrac{3X - 4Y}{5}\), \(\quad y = \dfrac{4X + 3Y}{5}\)

Check with the picture: \(X = 2\), \(Y = 1\) gives \(x = \tfrac{6 - 4}{5} = 0.4\) and \(y = \tfrac{8 + 3}{5} = 2.2\). ✓ Writing everything over 5 keeps the fractions tidy for the next step.

(c) Substitute — Rewrite the Equation in \(X\) and \(Y\)

The shortcut (spot it and save 10 minutes)

Look at the three squared terms. They form a perfect square:

$$ 9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2 = (3x + 4y)^2 $$

Check: \((3x + 4y)^2 = 9x^2 + 2\cdot 3x\cdot 4y + 16y^2 = 9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2\). ✓

And the other two terms share a factor:

$$ -30x - 40y = -10(3x + 4y) $$

So the whole equation only depends on the single combination \(3x + 4y\):

$$ (3x + 4y)^2 - 10(3x + 4y) = 0 $$

Now convert just that combination using part (b):

$$ 3x + 4y = 3\cdot\frac{3X - 4Y}{5} + 4\cdot\frac{4X + 3Y}{5} $$
$$ = \frac{9X - 12Y + 16X + 12Y}{5} = \frac{25X}{5} = 5X $$

The \(Y\)'s cancel! Put \(5X\) back in:

$$ (5X)^2 - 10(5X) = 0 $$
(c) \(25X^2 - 50X = 0\)

The long way (works for every question)

If you don't spot a shortcut, substitute each piece separately and add them up. Start with the three squared pieces:

$$ x^2 = \frac{(3X - 4Y)^2}{25} $$
$$ = \frac{9X^2 - 24XY + 16Y^2}{25} $$
$$ xy = \frac{(3X - 4Y)(4X + 3Y)}{25} $$
$$ = \frac{12X^2 - 7XY - 12Y^2}{25} $$
$$ y^2 = \frac{(4X + 3Y)^2}{25} $$
$$ = \frac{16X^2 + 24XY + 9Y^2}{25} $$

Multiply by 9, 24 and 16, and collect the coefficients in a table (everything is over 25):

term\(X^2\)\(XY\)\(Y^2\)
\(9x^2\)81−216144
\(24xy\)288−168−288
\(16y^2\)256384144
total ÷ 25625 ÷ 25 = 2500

Then the two plain terms:

$$ -30x = -30\cdot\frac{3X - 4Y}{5} = -18X + 24Y $$
$$ -40y = -40\cdot\frac{4X + 3Y}{5} = -32X - 24Y $$
$$ \text{together: } -50X + 0Y $$

Same answer: \(25X^2 + 0XY + 0Y^2 - 50X + 0Y = 0\), i.e. \(25X^2 - 50X = 0\). ✓

The \(XY\) column adding to 0 is the whole point of choosing \(\theta\) — it always happens. The \(Y^2\) column being 0 too is special to this question, and it's a clue: only one squared term left means "parabola family".

(d) Tidy Up — Complete the Square

From \(25X^2 - 50X = 0\) to a standard form

Divide everything by 25
$$ X^2 - 2X = 0 $$
Complete the square: half of −2 is −1, and \((-1)^2 = 1\) — add 1 to both sides
$$ X^2 - 2X + 1 = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (X - 1)^2 = 1 $$
Shift the origin to \(X = 1\)

Let \(X' = X - 1\) and \(Y' = Y\) (slide the origin 1 unit along the \(X\)-axis):

(d) \(X'^2 = 1\)

(e) Identify It — the Twist

The discriminant says "parabola"…

$$ B^2 - 4AC = 24^2 - 4(9)(16) = 576 - 576 = 0 $$

Zero means the parabola family. But compare with what a real parabola looks like in rotated axes:

$$ \text{real parabola: } X'^2 = 4a\,Y' \qquad \text{ours: } X'^2 = 1 $$

Ours has no \(Y'\) at all. So what is it? Solve it like a normal equation:

$$ X'^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; X' = 1 \;\text{ or }\; X' = -1 $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; X = 2 \;\text{ or }\; X = 0 $$

"\(X = 2\)" means every point that is 2 along the \(X\)-axis, whatever its \(Y\) — that's a straight line. Same for \(X = 0\). So the "conic" is two parallel straight lines.

The picture

To write them in \(x\) and \(y\), go back the other way. Earlier we found \(3x + 4y = 5X\), so \(X = \dfrac{3x + 4y}{5}\):

$$ X = 0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; 3x + 4y = 0 $$
$$ X = 2 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; 3x + 4y = 10 $$
(e) A degenerate parabola: the pair of parallel lines \(3x + 4y = 0\) and \(3x + 4y = 10\)

Double-check by factoring the original equation: \((3x + 4y)(3x + 4y - 10) = (3x+4y)^2 - 10(3x + 4y) = 9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2 - 30x - 40y\). ✓ A product equals 0 when either factor is 0 — two lines.

"Degenerate" just means the conic has collapsed into something simpler (here two lines instead of a curve). A cone sliced exactly parallel to its side can do this.

(f) Its Properties

What we can (and can't) say

  • No vertex, focus or directrix. Those only exist for a genuine parabola. Two straight lines don't have them — this is why (f) feels impossible.
  • Direction: both lines have slope \(-\tfrac34\) (rearrange \(3x + 4y = c\) to \(y = -\tfrac34 x + \tfrac{c}{4}\)), so they're parallel.
  • Distance apart: 2 units — from \(X = 0\) to \(X = 2\). Check with the point-to-line formula: \(\dfrac{|10 - 0|}{\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2}} = \dfrac{10}{5} = 2\).
  • Line of symmetry: the midline \(X = 1\), i.e. \(3x + 4y = 5\), exactly halfway between them.
Part (f) asks for a vertex, so the question was very likely meant to give a real parabola — probably a sign typo in the \(x\) and \(y\) terms. Your working for (a)–(d) is exactly the same either way; only the last step changes.

What it would look like with a real parabola

Change just the linear terms to \(9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2 - 80x + 60y = 0\). Steps (a) and (b) are identical. In (c) the squared part still gives \(25X^2\), and

$$ -80x + 60y = \frac{-80(3X - 4Y) + 60(4X + 3Y)}{5} $$
$$ = \frac{500Y}{5} = 100Y $$
$$ 25X^2 + 100Y = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; X^2 = -4Y $$

Now there is a \(Y\)-term, so it's a genuine parabola: \(4a = 4\), \(a = 1\), opening in the \(-Y\) direction.

In \(xy\)-coordinates: vertex \((0, 0)\); focus \((X, Y) = (0, -1)\) → \(x = \tfrac{0 + 4}{5} = 0.8\), \(y = \tfrac{0 - 3}{5} = -0.6\); directrix \(Y = 1\) → \(-4x + 3y = 5\).

The Exam Recipe

Any "rotate the conic" question, in order

  1. Read off \(A, B, C, D, E, F\) (\(B\) = the whole \(xy\) number).
  2. \(\cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B}\). If \(A = C\), \(\theta = 45^\circ\) — skip to step 4.
  3. Triangle → \(\cos 2\theta\) → half-angle formulas → \(\cos\theta\), \(\sin\theta\) (both positive).
  4. \(x = X\cos\theta - Y\sin\theta\), \(y = X\sin\theta + Y\cos\theta\).
  5. Substitute (look for a perfect-square shortcut first). The \(XY\)-term must vanish — if it doesn't, recheck.
  6. Complete the square, shift the origin, get a standard form.
  7. Identify (discriminant \(B^2 - 4AC\): \(<0\) ellipse, \(=0\) parabola, \(>0\) hyperbola), then read off the features.

Common confusions

Why is \(\cos 2\theta\) negative — did I mess up?
No. \(\cot 2\theta\) is negative whenever \(A < C\), which puts \(2\theta\) between \(90^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\). The half-angle formulas handle it.
Why do I take the positive square roots?
The rotation angle is always chosen between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\), where both \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) are positive.
Which way round are the transformation equations?
Old in terms of new: \(x = X\cos\theta - Y\sin\theta\). Picture it: going \(X\) units along the turned \(X\)-axis moves you \(X\cos\theta\) across and \(X\sin\theta\) up — that's the purple path in the "two addresses" picture.
How do I go back from \(X, Y\) to \(x, y\) at the end?
Use the reverse pair: \(X = x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta\), \(Y = -x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta\). Here \(X = \tfrac{3x + 4y}{5}\).
The discriminant is 0 but the answer isn't a parabola?
A discriminant of 0 means "parabola or a degenerate version of one" (two parallel lines, one line, or nothing). You only find out which after rotating and tidying.
