Part 1 — The Big Picture Unit 7

One Idea Behind All Three Theorems

All three theorems say the same thing in different dimensions: adding up a "derivative" over the inside of a region equals looking only at what happens on its edge.

TheoremInside (add up a derivative)Edge (boundary only)Shape
Green's\(\displaystyle\iint_R \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA\)\(\displaystyle\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy\)Flat region in the \(xy\)-plane
Stokes'\(\displaystyle\iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\)\(\displaystyle\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\)Curved surface with an edge
Divergence\(\displaystyle\iiint_G \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV\)\(\displaystyle\oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\)Solid with a closed skin
You already know the 1D version: \(\displaystyle\int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a)\). Inside = the derivative added up; edge = the two endpoints.

Which "derivative" goes inside?

  • Curl (spin) for Green's and Stokes' — because the edge integral measures circulation (flow around a loop).
  • Divergence (spreading) for the divergence theorem — because the edge integral measures flux (flow out through a skin).

Part 2 — Green's Theorem Unit 7

Statement from slides

If \(C\) is a simple closed curve traversed counterclockwise and \(R\) is the region inside it:

$$ \oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_R \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA $$
  • "Counterclockwise" = walking along \(C\) with the region on your left.
  • \(P\) is the coefficient of \(dx\); \(Q\) is the coefficient of \(dy\).
  • \(Q_x - P_y\) is the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the curl: how much the field spins at each point.
The method
  1. Check \(C\) is closed and counterclockwise (if clockwise, the answer flips sign)
  2. Read off \(P\) and \(Q\); compute \(\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}\)
  3. Describe the region \(R\) with limits (sketch it!) and do the double integral — polar if \(R\) is a disc
If \(Q_x - P_y\) is a constant \(k\), the answer is just \(k \times \text{area}(R)\) — no integration needed.
⚠ It's \(Q_x - P_y\) — derivative of the \(dy\)-part by \(x\), minus derivative of the \(dx\)-part by \(y\). Reversing them flips the sign.

Worked Examples from slides

Example — square \(0 \le x, y \le 1\)

\(\oint_C y^2\,dx + x^2\,dy\):  \(P = y^2\), \(Q = x^2\), so \(Q_x - P_y = 2x - 2y\).

$$ \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 (2x - 2y)\,dx\,dy $$
$$ = \int_0^1 (1 - 2y)\,dy = \big[y - y^2\big]_0^1 = 0 $$
Example 01 — triangle \((0,0), (1,0), (1,2)\)

\(\oint_C xy\,dx + x^2y^3\,dy\):  \(Q_x - P_y = 2xy^3 - x\). The slanted edge is \(y = 2x\), so \(0 \le x \le 1\), \(0 \le y \le 2x\).

$$ \int_0^{2x} (2xy^3 - x)\,dy $$
$$ = \left[\frac{xy^4}{2} - xy\right]_0^{2x} = 8x^5 - 2x^2 $$
$$ \int_0^1 (8x^5 - 2x^2)\,dx = \frac86 - \frac23 = \frac23 $$
Example 02 — rectangle \(0 \le x \le 2,\ 0 \le y \le 2\)

\(\oint_C (x^2 - y^2)\,dx + 2xy\,dy\):  \(Q_x - P_y = 2y - (-2y) = 4y\).

$$ \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^2 4y\,dy\,dx = \int_0^2 8\,dx = 16 $$
Example 03 — triangle \((0,0), (2,0), (2,2)\)

\(\oint_C (x^2 + y)\,dx + (x + y^2)\,dy\):  \(Q_x - P_y = 1 - 1 = 0\), so the answer is \(0\) — whatever the region.

When \(Q_x = P_y\), the field has no spin (irrotational) and the circulation around every closed curve is zero — exactly the conservative-field test from Unit 6.
From the review class
Triangle \((0,0), (1,0), (1,2)\): \(\oint x^2y\,dx + x\,dy\)
$$ \iint_R (1 - x^2)\,dA = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{2x} (1 - x^2)\,dy\,dx $$
$$ = \int_0^1 (2x - 2x^3)\,dx = \frac12 $$
Work around the unit circle: \(\mathbf{F} = (e^x - y^3)\mathbf{i} + (\cos y + x^3)\mathbf{j}\)

\(Q_x - P_y = 3x^2 + 3y^2 = 3r^2\) — the messy \(e^x\) and \(\cos y\) disappear. Switch to polar:

$$ W = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1 3r^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = 3\cdot\frac14\cdot 2\pi = \frac{3\pi}{2} $$

Part 3 — Flux Unit 7

What Flows Through a Surface from slides

Hold a butterfly net in a river. Flux measures how much water passes through the net per second. Only the part of the flow perpendicular to the net counts.

Constant field, flat surface of area \(A\)
$$ \text{Flux} = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,A = |\mathbf{F}|\,A\cos\theta $$
General case — add up tiny flat pieces
$$ \Phi = \iint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS $$
  • \(\mathbf{n}\) is the unit normal; its direction is the orientation ("oriented up", "outward").
  • Positive flux = net flow in the direction of \(\mathbf{n}\); negative = net flow against it.

Part 4 — Surface Integrals Review class · Textbook

The Area Element \(dS\) review class

A surface \(z = g(x,y)\) sits above a "shadow" region \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane. A tilted piece of surface is bigger than its shadow — the steeper it is, the bigger. The stretch factor is:

$$ dS = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right)^2 + 1}\;dA $$
Surface area
$$ \text{Area} = \iint_R \sqrt{z_x^2 + z_y^2 + 1}\;dA $$
Review-class example — paraboloid \(z = x^2 + y^2\) below \(z = 1\)

\(z_x = 2x\), \(z_y = 2y\), so the factor is \(\sqrt{4x^2 + 4y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{4r^2 + 1}\). The shadow is the disc \(r \le 1\).

$$ S = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1 \sqrt{4r^2 + 1}\;r\,dr\,d\theta $$

Substitute \(u = 4r^2 + 1\), \(du = 8r\,dr\) (so \(r\,dr = \tfrac18 du\)), with \(u\) from 1 to 5:

$$ S = 2\pi\cdot\frac18\int_1^5 \sqrt{u}\,du = \frac{\pi}{4}\cdot\frac23\Big[u^{3/2}\Big]_1^5 $$
$$ = \frac{\pi}{6}\left(5\sqrt5 - 1\right) \approx 5.33 $$

Integrating a Function Over a Surface textbook

$$ \iint_\sigma f(x,y,z)\,dS $$
$$ = \iint_R f\big(x, y, g(x,y)\big)\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\;dA $$
  1. Write the surface as \(z = g(x,y)\) and find its shadow \(R\)
  2. Replace every \(z\) in \(f\) by \(g(x,y)\)
  3. Multiply by the stretch factor \(\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\) and integrate over \(R\)
For a plane \(ax + by + cz = d\), the stretch factor is a constant: \(\dfrac{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}{|c|}\). E.g. \(2x + 3y + z = 6\) gives \(\sqrt{14}\).
⚠ "First octant" means \(x, y, z \ge 0\). For a plane, the shadow is the triangle cut off by the axes — set \(z = 0\) in the plane's equation to find its slanted edge.

Flux Through a Curved Surface \(z = g(x,y)\) textbook

For \(\mathbf{F} = P\mathbf{i} + Q\mathbf{j} + R\mathbf{k}\) and the surface oriented upward:

$$ \iint_\sigma \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS $$
$$ = \iint_R \left(-P\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} - Q\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial y} + R\right) dA $$

Where it comes from: the upward normal to \(z = g(x,y)\) is \(\mathbf{N} = \langle -g_x,\ -g_y,\ 1\rangle\) (it points up because its \(\mathbf{k}\)-part is \(+1\)), and \(\mathbf{n}\,dS = \mathbf{N}\,dA\). So the integrand is just \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{N}\). For downward orientation, flip the sign.

  1. Write \(g\), compute \(g_x\) and \(g_y\)
  2. Form \(-Pg_x - Qg_y + R\), replacing \(z\) by \(g(x,y)\)
  3. Integrate over the shadow \(R\) — usually polar, since shadows of paraboloids and spheres are discs
Sanity-check example — vertical rain

\(\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{k}\) (straight up) through any upward-oriented surface \(z = g(x,y)\): the integrand is \(0 + 0 + 1 = 1\), so the flux is the area of the shadow. That makes sense: vertical rain through a roof depends only on how much ground the roof covers, not how it's shaped.

Part 5 — The Divergence Theorem Unit 7

Statement from slides

For a solid \(G\) with closed surface \(S\), oriented outward:

$$ \oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_G \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\;dV $$
Total flux out through the skin = total divergence (sources minus sinks) inside.
The method
  1. Make sure the surface is closed (hemisphere + base, cylinder + top + bottom, box, …)
  2. Compute \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = P_x + Q_y + R_z\) — usually much simpler than \(\mathbf{F}\)
  3. Set up \(\iiint_G \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV\) in the coordinates that fit the solid: box → Cartesian, cylinder/paraboloid → cylindrical, ball → spherical
If \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\) is a constant \(k\): flux \(= k \times \text{volume}(G)\).
⚠ Only for closed surfaces. "The surface of the region enclosed by \(z = \ldots\) and \(z = 0\)" means dome plus base — that's closed. A dome on its own is not.

Worked Examples from slides

Example 1 — box \(0 \le x \le 1,\ 0 \le y \le 2,\ 0 \le z \le 1\)

\(\mathbf{F} = x^2\mathbf{i} + 2yz\,\mathbf{j} + 3z^2\mathbf{k}\):  \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 2x + 2z + 6z = 2x + 8z\).

$$ \int_0^1 (2x + 8z)\,dz = 2x + 4 $$
$$ \int_0^2 (2x + 4)\,dy = 4x + 8 $$
$$ \Phi = \int_0^1 (4x + 8)\,dx = 2 + 8 = 10 $$
Example 2 — unit cube

\(\mathbf{F} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}\):  \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 3\), a constant, so \(\Phi = 3 \times \text{volume} = 3 \times 1 = 3\).

Doing it the long way would mean six separate surface integrals, one per face — the theorem turns that into one line.

Part 6 — Stokes' Theorem Unit 7

Statement from slides

$$ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\;dS $$
  • \(C\) is the boundary (edge) of the surface \(S\).
  • \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\) is the curl; \(\mathbf{n}\) is the unit normal to \(S\).
Circulation around the edge = total curl (spin) through the surface.

Green's theorem is Stokes' theorem for a flat surface in the \(xy\)-plane: there \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{k}\), and \((\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k} = Q_x - P_y\).

Any surface with the same edge gives the same answer — so you can always choose the easiest one (often the flat disc).

Worked Example from slides

\(\mathbf{F} = -y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\), \(C\): circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\), \(z = 0\), counterclockwise
$$ \nabla\times\mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z \\ -y & x & 0 \end{vmatrix} = 2\mathbf{k} $$

Choose \(S\) = the flat disc, so \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{k}\) and \((\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n} = 2\):

$$ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S 2\,dS = 2\times\pi(2)^2 = 8\pi $$

Check directly: \(x = 2\cos t\), \(y = 2\sin t\) gives \(\int_0^{2\pi}(4\sin^2 t + 4\cos^2 t)\,dt = 8\pi\). ✓

Part 7 — Which Theorem? + Common Mistakes

Reading the question

You see…Use
\(\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy\), closed curve in the planeGreen's theorem
A line integral along a path that isn't closedParametrise — or close it, use Green, subtract the added piece
\(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) around a loop in 3DStokes' theorem (or parametrise)
"outward flux" through a closed surfaceDivergence theorem
Flux through an open surface \(z = g(x,y)\)\(\iint_R(-Pg_x - Qg_y + R)\,dA\)
\(\iint_\sigma f\,dS\)\(dS = \sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\,dA\)
"surface area" of a curved surface\(\iint_R\sqrt{z_x^2 + z_y^2 + 1}\,dA\)

The mistakes that actually cost marks

  1. Green's order: \(Q_x - P_y\), not \(P_y - Q_x\).
  2. Orientation: clockwise curves and downward/inward normals flip the sign.
  3. Divergence theorem on an open surface — it needs a closed skin.
  4. Forgetting the stretch factor \(\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\) in \(dS\).
  5. Leaving \(z\) in the integrand — on the surface, \(z = g(x,y)\); substitute it.
  6. Forgetting the Jacobian (\(r\) or \(\rho^2\sin\phi\)) when the triple integral switches coordinates.

Ten-second checks

  • Constant integrand? The answer is constant × area (Green) or constant × volume (divergence).
  • Flux of \(\mathbf{k}\) upward through any graph = area of its shadow.
  • Hemisphere volume \(\tfrac23\pi a^3\), cylinder \(\pi r^2 h\), paraboloid cap \(z = k - r^2\): \(\tfrac{\pi k^2}{2}\).
