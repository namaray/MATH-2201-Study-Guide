Part 1 — Why Change Coordinates? Unit 5

A Coordinate System Is a Language for Locating Points from slides

The same region can have ugly limits in one system and constant limits in another. A disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le a^2\) needs \(y\) from \(-\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\) to \(\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\) in Cartesian coordinates — but in polar it's simply \(0 \le r \le a\), \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\).

"Good coordinates don't change the problem — they make the problem easier to see." — Unit 5 slides

Part 2 — Polar Coordinates Unit 5

Definition & Conversions from slides

A point is located by \(r\), its distance from the origin (the pole), and \(\theta\), the angle measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis.

Polar → CartesianCartesian → Polar
\(x = r\cos\theta\)\(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\)
\(y = r\sin\theta\)\(\tan\theta = \dfrac{y}{x}\)  (then fix the quadrant)
\(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\)  — the identity you'll use most
Getting \(\theta\) into the right quadrant

\(\tan^{-1}\) only ever returns angles between \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), so it can't tell \((1,1)\) from \((-1,-1)\). The safe method: find the reference angle \(\alpha = \tan^{-1}\left|\tfrac{y}{x}\right|\), then place it:

QuadrantSigns\(\theta\)
I\(x > 0,\ y > 0\)\(\alpha\)
II\(x < 0,\ y > 0\)\(\pi - \alpha\)
III\(x < 0,\ y < 0\)\(\pi + \alpha\)
IV\(x > 0,\ y < 0\)\(2\pi - \alpha\)
Quick example

Convert \((-1, \sqrt3)\) to polar. \(r = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2\). Reference angle \(\alpha = \tan^{-1}\sqrt3 = \tfrac{\pi}{3}\).

\(x < 0\), \(y > 0\) → Quadrant II → \(\theta = \pi - \tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\). Answer: \(\left(2, \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)\).

⚠ Typing \(\tan^{-1}(\sqrt3 / -1)\) into a calculator gives \(-\tfrac{\pi}{3}\) — a point in Quadrant IV. Always sketch where the point is first.

Converting Equations

Substitute \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\), and \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\), then simplify.

CartesianPolarCurve
\(x^2 + y^2 = a^2\)\(r = a\)Circle, radius \(a\), centred at origin
\(y = x\tan\alpha\)\(\theta = \alpha\)Line through the origin
\(x^2 + y^2 = 2ax\)\(r = 2a\cos\theta\)Circle, radius \(a\), centre \((a, 0)\)
\(x^2 + y^2 = 2ay\)\(r = 2a\sin\theta\)Circle, radius \(a\), centre \((0, a)\)

Worked example — Exercise 02 from slides

Convert \((x^2 + y^2 - 2x)^2 = 4(x^2 + y^2)\) to polar.

$$ (r^2 - 2r\cos\theta)^2 = 4r^2 $$
$$ r^2(r - 2\cos\theta)^2 = 4r^2 $$
$$ \;\Longrightarrow\; (r - 2\cos\theta)^2 = 4 $$
$$ r - 2\cos\theta = 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; r = 2(1 + \cos\theta) $$

A cardioid. (Its area is worked out in Part 4.)

Part 3 — The Jacobian Unit 5

What It Is and Why You Need It from slides

The Jacobian tells you how much a tiny area (or volume) gets stretched or compressed when you change coordinate systems. A small polar patch \(dr \times d\theta\) is not a square — its width grows with \(r\) — so \(dA\) can't just be \(dr\,d\theta\).

$$ J = \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = \begin{vmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\[2mm] \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v} \end{vmatrix} $$
$$ dx\,dy = |J|\,du\,dv $$
⚠ Use the absolute value \(|J|\). Area can't be negative, but a determinant can.
Derivation for polar — the most important one

With \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\):

$$ \frac{\partial x}{\partial r} = \cos\theta,\qquad \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} = -r\sin\theta $$
$$ \frac{\partial y}{\partial r} = \sin\theta,\qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta} = r\cos\theta $$
$$ J = \begin{vmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{vmatrix} = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r $$
\(dA = dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta\). That extra \(r\) is the Jacobian — never drop it.
Worked example — a non-polar substitution from slides

Find the Jacobian when \(x = uv\), \(y = \dfrac{u}{v}\).

$$ \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} = v,\qquad \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} = u $$
$$ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} = \frac{1}{v},\qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} = -\frac{u}{v^2} $$
$$ J = \begin{vmatrix} v & u \\ \frac1v & -\frac{u}{v^2} \end{vmatrix} $$
$$ = v\left(-\frac{u}{v^2}\right) - u\left(\frac1v\right) $$
$$ = -\frac{u}{v} - \frac{u}{v} = -\frac{2u}{v} $$
$$ dx\,dy = \left|-\frac{2u}{v}\right| du\,dv $$
$$ = \frac{2u}{v}\,du\,dv \quad (u, v > 0) $$

Part 4 — Double Integrals in Polar Unit 5

The Conversion

$$ \iint_R f(x, y)\,dA $$
$$ = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{r_1(\theta)}^{r_2(\theta)} f(r\cos\theta,\, r\sin\theta)\; r\,dr\,d\theta $$
Area of a region
$$ A = \iint_R r\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ \text{and if } 0 \le r \le f(\theta): \quad A = \frac12\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} [f(\theta)]^2\,d\theta $$
The method
  1. Sketch the region; convert its boundary curves to polar
  2. \(r\) limits: from where a ray from the origin enters the region to where it leaves
  3. \(\theta\) limits: the range of angles that sweep out the whole region
  4. Replace \(dA\) with \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) — don't forget the \(r\)
  5. Integrate \(r\) first, then \(\theta\)
Trig integrals you'll need
$$ \cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2} $$
$$ \sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2} $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \sin^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi $$
$$ \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta = 0 $$

Worked Examples

Example 1 — area of a circle, two ways from slides

Region: \(x^2 + y^2 \le a^2\).

Cartesian: \(\displaystyle A = \int_{-a}^{a}\int_{-\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}^{\sqrt{a^2-x^2}} dy\,dx\) — correct, but the integral needs a trig substitution.

Polar: constant limits, easy integral:

$$ A = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{a} r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{a^2}{2}\,d\theta = \pi a^2 $$
Example 2 — an off-centre circle (Exercise 01) from slides

Evaluate \(\iint_R 1\,dA\) where \(R: x^2 + y^2 \le 6x\).

Convert: \(r^2 \le 6r\cos\theta \Rightarrow r \le 6\cos\theta\). Since \(r \ge 0\), we need \(\cos\theta \ge 0\), so \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\).

$$ \iint_R 1\,dA = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{6\cos\theta} r\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \frac{36\cos^2\theta}{2}\,d\theta $$
$$ = 18\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = 18 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = 9\pi $$
Check: \(x^2 + y^2 = 6x\) is \((x-3)^2 + y^2 = 9\), a circle of radius 3, so its area is \(9\pi\). ✓
Example 3 — area of the cardioid (Exercise 02) from slides

Area enclosed by \(r = 2(1 + \cos\theta)\), from Part 2.

$$ A = \frac12\int_0^{2\pi} [2(1 + \cos\theta)]^2\,d\theta $$
$$ = 2\int_0^{2\pi} \left(1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta\right) d\theta $$
$$ = 2\,\big(2\pi + 0 + \pi\big) = 6\pi $$
⚠ The off-centre circle trap. For \(r = 2a\cos\theta\) the whole circle is traced once as \(\theta\) runs from \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) to \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). Using \(0\) to \(2\pi\) traces it twice and doubles your answer. For \(r = 2a\sin\theta\), use \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\).

Part 5 — Cylindrical Coordinates Unit 5

Polar in the \(xy\)-Plane, Plus a Height from slides

A point is \((r, \theta, z)\), where

  • \(r\) = perpendicular distance from the \(z\)-axis
  • \(\theta\) = angle counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis, in the \(xy\)-plane
  • \(z\) = height above (or below) the \(xy\)-plane — unchanged from Cartesian
Cylindrical → CartesianCartesian → Cylindrical
\(x = r\cos\theta\)\(r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\)
\(y = r\sin\theta\)\(\theta = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{y}{x}\) (fix the quadrant)
\(z = z\)\(z = z\)
Common surfaces
Cylindrical equationCartesianSurface
\(r = a\)\(x^2 + y^2 = a^2\)Cylinder of radius \(a\) around the \(z\)-axis
\(\theta = \alpha\)—Vertical half-plane
\(z = c\)\(z = c\)Horizontal plane
\(z = r^2\)\(z = x^2 + y^2\)Paraboloid
\(z = r\)\(z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\)Cone
\(r^2 + z^2 = a^2\)\(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2\)Sphere

The Cylindrical Jacobian from slides

$$ J = \frac{\partial(x, y, z)}{\partial(r, \theta, z)} = \begin{vmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} $$
$$ = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r $$
\(dV = dx\,dy\,dz = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\) — the same extra \(r\) as polar, because \(z\) doesn't stretch anything.
The method
  1. \(z\) limits first: from the bottom surface to the top surface, written in terms of \(r\) (and \(\theta\))
  2. Then \(r\) and \(\theta\): exactly the polar limits of the region's shadow on the \(xy\)-plane
  3. Integrand gets the extra factor \(r\)

Worked Examples

Example 1 — a cylinder from slides

Volume of \(x^2 + y^2 \le 9\), \(0 \le z \le 4\): \(0 \le r \le 3\), \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\), \(0 \le z \le 4\).

$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^3\!\!\int_0^4 r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^3 4r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} 18\,d\theta = 36\pi $$
Check: \(\pi r^2 h = \pi(9)(4) = 36\pi\). ✓
Example 2 — under a paraboloid from slides

Volume enclosed by \(z = x^2 + y^2\) and the cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\) (above the \(xy\)-plane).

Convert: \(z = r^2\) and \(r = 2\). Limits: \(0 \le z \le r^2\), \(0 \le r \le 2\), \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\).

$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^{r^2} r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 r^3\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} 4\,d\theta = 8\pi $$
Example 3 — an off-centre cylinder from slides

Volume inside \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4x\), between \(z = 0\) and \(z = 3\).

Convert: \(r \le 4\cos\theta\), with \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\).

$$ V = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{4\cos\theta}\!\!\int_0^3 r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = 3\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{4\cos\theta} r\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = \frac32\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} 16\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = 24 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = 12\pi $$
Check: the base is a circle of radius 2 (area \(4\pi\)) and the height is 3, so \(V = 12\pi\). ✓

Part 6 — Spherical Coordinates Unit 5

One Distance and Two Angles from slides

A point is \(P(\rho, \theta, \phi)\), where

  • \(\rho\) = distance from the origin — \(\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\)
  • \(\theta\) = azimuthal angle in the \(xy\)-plane, from the positive \(x\)-axis (same \(\theta\) as polar)
  • \(\phi\) = polar angle measured down from the positive \(z\)-axis
$$ \rho \ge 0, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi, \qquad 0 \le \phi \le \pi $$
⚠ \(\phi\) only goes to \(\pi\), not \(2\pi\). \(\phi = 0\) is straight up the \(z\)-axis, \(\phi = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\) is the \(xy\)-plane, \(\phi = \pi\) is straight down. Together with \(\theta\) going all the way round, that covers every direction exactly once.
Deriving the conversions (see the 3D diagram in Part 5)

In the right triangle \(O\)–\(Q\)–\(P\), the hypotenuse is \(\rho\) and the angle at the top is \(\phi\), so

$$ r = \rho\sin\phi, \qquad z = \rho\cos\phi $$

Then substitute \(r\) into \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\):

Spherical → CartesianCartesian → Spherical
\(x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\)\(\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\)
\(y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\)\(\theta = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{y}{x}\) (fix the quadrant)
\(z = \rho\cos\phi\)\(\phi = \cos^{-1}\dfrac{z}{\rho}\)
\(\phi = \cos^{-1}(z/\rho)\) never needs a quadrant fix: \(\cos^{-1}\) returns exactly the range \(0\) to \(\pi\) that \(\phi\) lives in.
Common surfaces
Spherical equationSurface
\(\rho = a\)Sphere of radius \(a\), centred at the origin
\(\phi = \alpha\)Cone with vertex at the origin (e.g. \(\phi = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\) is \(z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\))
\(\phi = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\)The \(xy\)-plane
\(\theta = \alpha\)Vertical half-plane

The Spherical Jacobian from slides

$$ J = \frac{\partial(x, y, z)}{\partial(\rho, \phi, \theta)} = \begin{vmatrix} \sin\phi\cos\theta & \rho\cos\phi\cos\theta & -\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta \\ \sin\phi\sin\theta & \rho\cos\phi\sin\theta & \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta \\ \cos\phi & -\rho\sin\phi & 0 \end{vmatrix} $$
$$ |J| = \rho^2\sin\phi $$
\(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\). Memorise this one — deriving it in the exam takes too long.
Worked example — volume of a ball from slides

Volume of \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le a^2\): \(0 \le \rho \le a\), \(0 \le \phi \le \pi\), \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\).

$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^{a} \rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$

All limits are constants and the integrand factors, so it splits into three separate integrals:

$$ \int_0^{a}\rho^2\,d\rho = \frac{a^3}{3} $$
$$ \int_0^{\pi}\sin\phi\,d\phi = \big[-\cos\phi\big]_0^{\pi} = 2 $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}d\theta = 2\pi $$
$$ V = \frac{a^3}{3}\cdot 2 \cdot 2\pi = \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 \;\checkmark $$
Half the ball (above the \(xy\)-plane) just changes \(\phi\) to \(0 \le \phi \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\), which makes \(\int\sin\phi\,d\phi = 1\) instead of 2 — exactly half the volume.

Part 8 — Exam Strategy & Common Mistakes

The mistakes that actually cost marks

  1. Dropping the Jacobian — the \(r\) in polar and cylindrical, the \(\rho^2\sin\phi\) in spherical. This is the most common single error in the whole unit.
  2. \(\theta\) in the wrong quadrant — \(\tan^{-1}\) can't tell Quadrant II from IV. Sketch the point.
  3. \(\phi\) from 0 to \(2\pi\) — it stops at \(\pi\).
  4. Off-centre circle limits — \(r = 2a\cos\theta\) uses \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  5. Mixing up \(r\) and \(\rho\) — \(r\) is distance from the \(z\)-axis; \(\rho\) is distance from the origin. \(r = \rho\sin\phi\).
  6. Swapping \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) — in your course \(\theta\) is around the \(z\)-axis and \(\phi\) is down from it. Some books and physics courses swap them; stick to the slides' convention.
  7. "Above the \(xy\)-plane" means \(0 \le \phi \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\), not a change to \(\theta\).
  8. Negative Jacobian — take the absolute value.

Ten-second checks

  • Converted a point? Check \(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\) is the same before and after.
  • Volume of a cylinder should be \(\pi r^2 h\); a ball \(\tfrac43\pi a^3\); a hemisphere \(\tfrac23\pi a^3\).
  • Area of a region bounded by a circle should be \(\pi(\text{radius})^2\) — find the radius by completing the square.
  • Your final answer should have no \(r\), \(\theta\), \(\rho\), or \(\phi\) left in it.
