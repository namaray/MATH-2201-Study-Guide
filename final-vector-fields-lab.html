1. Gradient & directional derivative

The colours and lines are a map of \(f\) — like height on a hill. Drag the point \(P\). The green arrow \(\nabla f\) always points straight uphill, across the contour lines.

Drag anywhere to move \(P\). Red = high, blue = low.

Steepest rate \(|\nabla f|\)
Angle between \(\mathbf{u}\) and \(\nabla f\)
\(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}\)
The idea \(D_{\mathbf{u}}f = |\nabla f|\cos\alpha\): the "shadow" of \(\nabla f\) on your direction (orange). Facing uphill (\(\alpha = 0\)) gives the biggest rate, \(|\nabla f|\); walking along a contour (\(\alpha = 90^\circ\)) gives 0; facing downhill gives \(-|\nabla f|\).
Try this
  • Pick \(x^2 + 3xy\), drag \(P\) to \((1, 2)\) and set \(\mathbf{u}\) to 53° (that's \(\langle 3, 4\rangle/5\)): you should get 7.2, the notes' Example 2.
  • Drag \(P\) across the map: \(\nabla f\) is always perpendicular to the contour through \(P\).

2. Divergence & curl — spreading and spinning

Tiny particles drift with the field. Drag the probe (the circle) around: its arrows show flow in or out (divergence) and its paddle wheel turns with the local spin (curl).

Drag to move the probe. Red edge arrows: flowing out. Blue: flowing in.

Probe centre
\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\) at the probe
\((\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{k}\) at the probe
What it means
The idea
  • Divergence = net outflow per unit area. Positive: more leaves the probe than enters (a source). Negative: a sink. Zero: what goes in comes out.
  • Curl = how fast a tiny paddle wheel would spin. Positive: counterclockwise. Zero: no spin (irrotational).
Try this
  • Shear: every arrow points the same way, yet the wheel spins — curl isn't about curved arrows, it's about neighbouring arrows having different speeds.
  • \(x^2\mathbf{i} + 3xy\,\mathbf{j}\) (the notes' Question 1, slice \(z = 0\)): move the probe left and right and watch the divergence \(5x\) change sign.

3. Work along a path — when the path doesn't matter

Conservative?
The idea If \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla\phi\) (test: \(P_y = Q_x\)), the work is just \(\phi(B) - \phi(A)\) — every path gives the same number. For \(-y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\), \(P_y = -1 \ne 1 = Q_x\): the field swirls, and different paths collect different amounts.

4. Green's theorem — edge circulation = total spin inside

Colour shows the spin \(Q_x - P_y\) at each point. Adding it up over the region gives exactly the work done going once round the edge (counterclockwise).

Little circles: the spin of each small cell. Neighbouring cells' edges cancel; only the outer edge is left.

\(Q_x - P_y\)
\(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) (round the edge)
\(\iint_R (Q_x - P_y)\,dA\) (inside)
The idea Each little cell's circulation is about (its spin) × (its area). Where two cells touch, they walk the shared edge in opposite directions, so those parts cancel. Add up every cell and only the outer edge survives — that's Green's theorem.
Try this
  • \(y^2\mathbf{i} + x^2\mathbf{j}\) on the square centred at the origin: red and blue halves cancel → 0.
  • \((x^2 + y)\mathbf{i} + (x + y^2)\mathbf{j}\): no spin anywhere, so every region gives 0 (it's conservative).
