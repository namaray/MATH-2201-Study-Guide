1. Flux through a patch — why the \(\cos\theta\)

Rain falls straight up through a small window (just to keep the arrows pointing up). Tilt the window: less rain gets through, even though the window is the same size.

Drag to rotate. Green arrow: the window's normal \(\mathbf{n}\). Red: the field \(\mathbf{F}\).

Window area \(A\)1.00
\(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n} = |\mathbf{F}|\cos\theta\)
Flux \(= \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,A\)
Meaning
The idea Flux only counts the part of \(\mathbf{F}\) that goes through the surface — the part along \(\mathbf{n}\). Face-on: all of it. Edge-on (\(90^\circ\)): none. Past \(90^\circ\) the flow goes through the "wrong" way, so the flux is negative.

A small square \(dA\) on the floor (green) sits under a patch \(dS\) of the surface (orange). The steeper the surface, the bigger \(dS\) is compared with its shadow.

\(g_x,\ g_y\) here
\(\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\)
Area of \(dS\) ÷ area of \(dA\)
The idea Flat surface: factor 1 (no stretch). A tilted plane: the same factor everywhere (it's a constant — plane questions are easy). The bowl: flat at the bottom, steeper further out, so the factor \(\sqrt{4r^2 + 1}\) grows with \(r\).

3. The divergence theorem — outflow = total source inside

Colour on the closed surface shows \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\): red where the field leaves, blue where it enters. The total outflow always matches the sum of the divergence inside.

Drag to rotate.

\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\)
Outward flux \(\oiint \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\) (surface)
\(\iiint \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\,dV\) (inside)
The idea Every bit of "source" inside (positive divergence) has to leave through the skin. So you can measure the outflow by adding up the sources instead — one triple integral instead of a surface integral over every face.
Try this
  • \(x\,\mathbf{i} + y\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\): divergence 3, so flux = 3 × volume. Double the size: the flux goes up 8 times.
  • \(-y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\) just swirls: red and blue cancel and the flux is 0.
  • Shift the centre: for constant divergence nothing changes; for \(x^3\mathbf{i} + \dots\) it does.

4. Stokes' theorem — any surface with the same edge

The edge \(C\) is the circle of radius 2. Inflate the surface from a flat disc into a dome: the flux of the curl through it never changes, because it always equals the circulation around \(C\).

Drag to rotate. Green arrows: \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\). Purple: the edge \(C\), counterclockwise.

\(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\)
\(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) (round the edge)
\(\iint_S (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS\) (through the dome)
The idea The circulation only depends on the edge, so any surface with that edge gives the same flux of the curl. In exams, pick the easiest one — usually the flat disc, where \(\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{k}\) and you only need the \(\mathbf{k}\)-part of the curl. For \(-y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j}\) that gives \(2 \times \pi(2)^2 = 8\pi \approx 25.13\).
