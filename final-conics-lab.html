1. One rule, three curves — focus, directrix & eccentricity

Every conic is the set of points \(P\) with \(PF = e \cdot PD\): distance to a focus \(F\) is \(e\) times the distance to a directrix line. Change \(e\) and watch the shape change type.

Orange: \(PF\) (to the focus). Purple: \(PD\) (to the directrix).

Type
\(PF\) (to the focus)
\(PD\) (to the directrix)
\(PF \div PD\)
The idea
  • \(e < 1\): \(P\) must stay closer to \(F\) than to the line → a closed curve, an ellipse.
  • \(e = 1\): equally far → a parabola.
  • \(e > 1\): further from \(F\) than from the line is allowed → two branches, a hyperbola.
Try this
  • Move \(P\) around: the ratio stays exactly \(e\).
  • Slide \(e\) slowly through 1 and watch the ellipse open up into a parabola, then split into a hyperbola.

2. The string property — ellipse sum, hyperbola difference

An ellipse is where the two focal distances add to \(2a\); a hyperbola is where they differ by \(2a\). That is where \(c^2 = a^2 - b^2\) and \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2\) come from.

Orange: \(PF_1\). Green: \(PF_2\).

Equation
Focal distance \(c\)
\(PF_1\), \(PF_2\)
\(PF_1 + PF_2\)
Eccentricity \(e = c/a\)

3. Shifting a conic — standard form ↔ general form

Moving a conic so its centre (or vertex) is at \((h, k)\) replaces \(x\) with \(x - h\) and \(y\) with \(y - k\). Expanding gives the messy general form you see in exam questions — completing the square undoes it.

Grey dashed: the same conic at the origin. Green arrow: the shift \((h, k)\).

Standard form
Expanded (general) form
Centre / vertex
The idea The default settings give \(4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 18y - 11 = 0\) — the worked example in the notes. Read it backwards: group the \(x\)-terms and the \(y\)-terms, complete each square, and the numbers inside the brackets tell you \(h\) and \(k\).
Try this
  • Set \(h = k = 0\): the \(x\) and \(y\) terms (the \(D\) and \(E\) coefficients) vanish.
  • Change only \(h\): only the \(x\)-term and the constant change.

4. Rotation of axes — killing the \(xy\)-term

An \(xy\)-term means the conic is tilted. Rotating the axes by \(\theta\) with \(\cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B}\) lines them up with the conic, and the \(xy\)-term disappears.

Purple dashed: the rotated \(x'\)- and \(y'\)-axes.

Equation
\(B^2 - 4AC\) → type
Rotation \(\theta\)
In \(x'y'\) (no \(xy\)-term)
The idea The curve itself never moves — only the axes turn. In the new axes the equation is \(A'x'^2 + C'y'^2 = \text{const}\), and you can read off the conic as usual. Check: \(A' + C' = A + C\) always.
Try this
  • Make \(A = C\): the rotation is always \(45^\circ\).
  • Change \(B\) through the value where \(B^2 = 4AC\) and watch the ellipse stretch into a parabola-like strip, then a hyperbola.
