Q1. Scalar Field, Vector Field, or Invalid? 3 marks

Tap to enlarge
What you need to know
  • A scalar field gives one number at each point.
  • A vector field gives an arrow (components along \(\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}\)) at each point.
  • \(\nabla f\) (gradient) needs a scalar \(f\) and gives a vector. \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\) (divergence) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\) (curl) need a vector \(\mathbf{F}\).
The idea Ask two questions for each one: "is the output a number or an arrow?" and "is every operation being fed the right kind of input?"
(a) \(f(x,y,z) = \sin(xy) + \ln z\)

Put in a point, e.g. \((1, 0, 1)\): \(\sin 0 + \ln 1 = 0\). The output is a single number, with no \(\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}\).

(a) Scalar field — it gives one number at each point (defined where \(z > 0\), because of \(\ln z\)).
(b) \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = x^2\,\mathbf{i} - \cos y\,\mathbf{j} + e^z\,\mathbf{k}\)

It has three components, one along each of \(\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}\). At \((1, 0, 0)\) it is \(\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} + \mathbf{k}\): an arrow.

(b) Vector field — it gives a vector at each point (defined everywhere).
(c) \(g(x,y,z) = \nabla\cdot\left(x^2 + y^2\right)\)

The dot \(\nabla\cdot\) means divergence, which only works on a vector field: it needs components \(P, Q, R\) to compute \(P_x + Q_y + R_z\). But \(x^2 + y^2\) is a scalar — a single number at each point, with no components. There is nothing to dot with.

(c) Undefined / invalid — divergence can't be taken of a scalar function.
What would be valid Without the dot it's fine: the gradient \(\nabla\left(x^2 + y^2\right) = 2x\,\mathbf{i} + 2y\,\mathbf{j}\) is a vector field. And \(\nabla\cdot\left(\nabla(x^2 + y^2)\right) = 2 + 2 = 4\) is a scalar. The rule: gradient takes a scalar; divergence and curl take a vector.

Q2. Gradient of a Scalar Function 3 marks

Tap to enlarge
Formula you need
$$ \nabla\phi = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\,\mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\,\mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\,\mathbf{k} $$
The idea Take the three partial derivatives — each one treats the other two letters as constants — and stack them into a vector. Then put in the point.
Step 1 — \(\partial\phi/\partial x\) (treat \(y\), \(z\) as constants)

\(x^2yz \to 2x\,yz\) and \(3xy^2 \to 3y^2\):

$$ \phi_x = 2xyz + 3y^2 $$
Step 2 — \(\partial\phi/\partial y\) (treat \(x\), \(z\) as constants)

\(x^2yz \to x^2z\) and \(3xy^2 \to 3x \cdot 2y = 6xy\):

$$ \phi_y = x^2z + 6xy $$
Step 3 — \(\partial\phi/\partial z\) (treat \(x\), \(y\) as constants)

\(x^2yz \to x^2y\), and \(3xy^2\) has no \(z\), so it gives 0:

$$ \phi_z = x^2y $$
Step 4 — The gradient field
$$ \nabla\phi = \left(2xyz + 3y^2\right)\mathbf{i} + \left(x^2z + 6xy\right)\mathbf{j} $$
$$ \qquad + \; x^2y\,\mathbf{k} $$
Step 5 — Evaluate at \(P(1, 2, 1)\): \(x = 1\), \(y = 2\), \(z = 1\)
$$ \phi_x = 2(1)(2)(1) + 3(2)^2 = 4 + 12 = 16 $$
$$ \phi_y = (1)^2(1) + 6(1)(2) = 1 + 12 = 13 $$
$$ \phi_z = (1)^2(2) = 2 $$
\(\nabla\phi(1, 2, 1) = 16\,\mathbf{i} + 13\,\mathbf{j} + 2\,\mathbf{k}\)
What it means Standing at \(P\), \(\phi\) increases fastest in the direction \(\langle 16, 13, 2\rangle\), at a rate of \(\sqrt{256 + 169 + 4} = \sqrt{429} \approx 20.7\) per unit distance.

Q3. Directional Derivative Towards a Point 4 marks

Tap to enlarge
Formula you need
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = \nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}, \qquad \mathbf{u} = \frac{\vec{PQ}}{\|\vec{PQ}\|} $$
The idea The directional derivative is the slope of \(f\) if you walk from \(P\) in one particular direction. The direction must be a unit vector — so turn "towards \(Q\)" into the vector \(\vec{PQ}\), then divide by its length.
Step 1 — Partial derivatives

\(f_x\): \(e^{2y}\) is a constant, \(x^2 \to 2x\). \(f_y\): \(x^2\) is a constant, and by the chain rule \(e^{2y} \to 2e^{2y}\).

$$ f_x = 2x\,e^{2y}, \qquad f_y = 2x^2e^{2y} $$
Step 2 — Gradient at \(P(2, 0)\)

\(e^{2(0)} = e^0 = 1\), which keeps things simple:

$$ f_x(2, 0) = 2(2)(1) = 4 $$
$$ f_y(2, 0) = 2(4)(1) = 8 $$
$$ \nabla f(2, 0) = 4\,\mathbf{i} + 8\,\mathbf{j} $$
Step 3 — The direction vector \(\vec{PQ}\)

End minus start:

$$ \vec{PQ} = Q - P = (5 - 2,\ 4 - 0) = (3, 4) $$
Step 4 — Make it a unit vector
$$ \|\vec{PQ}\| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5 $$
$$ \mathbf{u} = \frac{(3, 4)}{5} = \left(\frac35, \frac45\right) $$
Step 5 — Dot product
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}f = (4)\left(\frac35\right) + (8)\left(\frac45\right) $$
$$ = \frac{12}{5} + \frac{32}{5} = \frac{44}{5} $$
\(D_{\mathbf{u}}f(2, 0) = \dfrac{44}{5} = 8.8\)
What it means — and a sanity check Walking from \(P\) towards \(Q\), \(f\) rises at about 8.8 units per unit distance. No direction can beat the gradient's length \(\sqrt{16 + 64} \approx 8.94\), and \(8.8 < 8.94\) ✓. Common mistake: using \((3, 4)\) without dividing by 5 gives 44 — five times too big.

Q4. Tangent Plane to a Surface 3 marks

Tap to enlarge
Formula you need For a surface \(F(x, y, z) = k\), the gradient \(\nabla F\) at \(P(x_0, y_0, z_0)\) is a normal vector, and the tangent plane is
$$ F_x(x - x_0) + F_y(y - y_0) + F_z(z - z_0) = 0 $$
The idea The gradient is always perpendicular to a level surface. A plane is fixed by one point and one perpendicular (normal) vector — the point is \(P\), the normal is \(\nabla F(P)\).
Step 1 — Write the surface as \(F = \text{constant}\)
$$ F(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 4 $$

Check \(P\) is on it: \(9 + 4 - 9 = 4\) ✓.

Step 2 — The gradient
$$ \nabla F = 2x\,\mathbf{i} + 2y\,\mathbf{j} - 2z\,\mathbf{k} $$
Step 3 — The normal at \(P(3, 2, 3)\)
$$ \nabla F(3, 2, 3) = 6\,\mathbf{i} + 4\,\mathbf{j} - 6\,\mathbf{k} $$
Step 4 — Plug into the plane formula
$$ 6(x - 3) + 4(y - 2) - 6(z - 3) = 0 $$
Step 5 — Expand and simplify
$$ 6x - 18 + 4y - 8 - 6z + 18 = 0 $$
$$ 6x + 4y - 6z = 8 $$

Every number is even, so divide by 2:

Tangent plane: \(3x + 2y - 3z = 4\)
Check Put \(P\) into the answer: \(3(3) + 2(2) - 3(3) = 9 + 4 - 9 = 4\) ✓ — the plane passes through \(P\). Its normal \(\langle 3, 2, -3\rangle\) is \(\tfrac12\nabla F(P)\), so it's perpendicular to the surface ✓.

Q5. Line Integral Along a Straight Segment Line integral

Tap to enlarge
Formula you need Parametrise \(C\) as \(x = x(t)\), \(y = y(t)\), \(a \le t \le b\), then
$$ \int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy $$
$$ = \int_a^b \Big(P\,x'(t) + Q\,y'(t)\Big)\,dt $$
The idea Turn everything into \(t\): replace \(x\), \(y\), \(dx\), \(dy\) using the parametrisation, and the line integral becomes an ordinary one-variable integral. (Here \(P_y = x\) but \(Q_x = 2x\), so the field is not conservative — the path matters, and we have to follow it.)
Step 1 — Parametrise the segment

The line through \((0,0)\) and \((2,4)\) has slope \(4/2 = 2\), so \(y = 2x\). Let \(x = t\); \(x\) goes from 0 to 2, so \(t\) does too.

$$ x = t, \qquad y = 2t, \qquad 0 \le t \le 2 $$
Step 2 — \(dx\) and \(dy\)
$$ dx = dt, \qquad dy = 2\,dt $$
Step 3 — Substitute, one piece at a time
$$ xy\,dx = (t)(2t)\,dt = 2t^2\,dt $$
$$ x^2\,dy = (t^2)(2\,dt) = 2t^2\,dt $$
$$ xy\,dx + x^2\,dy = 4t^2\,dt $$
Step 4 — Integrate from \(t = 0\) to \(t = 2\)
$$ \int_0^2 4t^2\,dt = \left[\frac{4t^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = \frac{32}{3} - 0 $$
\(\displaystyle\int_C \left(xy\,dx + x^2\,dy\right) = \frac{32}{3} \approx 10.67\)
Check with a different parametrisation \(x = 2t\), \(y = 4t\), \(0 \le t \le 1\): \(dx = 2\,dt\), \(dy = 4\,dt\).
$$ xy\,dx = (8t^2)(2\,dt) = 16t^2\,dt $$
$$ x^2\,dy = (4t^2)(4\,dt) = 16t^2\,dt $$
$$ \text{sum} = 32t^2\,dt $$
$$ \int_0^1 32t^2\,dt = \frac{32}{3} \;✓ $$
Any parametrisation of the same path in the same direction gives the same answer.

Q6. Conservative Field and Its Potential 7 marks

Tap to enlarge
Formula you need \(\mathbf{F} = P\,\mathbf{i} + Q\,\mathbf{j}\) (with continuous partials on the whole plane) is conservative exactly when
$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} $$
The idea If \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla f\), then \(P = f_x\) and \(Q = f_y\), so \(P_y = f_{xy}\) and \(Q_x = f_{yx}\) — and those are always equal. That's the test. Then we rebuild \(f\) by integrating \(P\) and fixing the leftover "constant" with \(Q\).

(a) Show that F is conservative

Step 1 — Read off \(P\) and \(Q\)
$$ P = 2xy + 3, \qquad Q = x^2 - 4y $$
Step 2 — \(\partial P/\partial y\) (treat \(x\) as a constant)

\(2xy \to 2x\), and the constant 3 gives 0:

$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 2x $$
Step 3 — \(\partial Q/\partial x\) (treat \(y\) as a constant)

\(x^2 \to 2x\), and \(-4y\) has no \(x\), so it gives 0:

$$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 2x $$
Step 4 — Compare
$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 2x = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} $$
\(P_y = Q_x\), and \(P\), \(Q\) are polynomials (smooth on the whole plane), so \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative.

(b) Find the potential function

Step 1 — Integrate \(P\) with respect to \(x\)

\(f_x = P\), so undo the \(x\)-derivative, holding \(y\) constant:

$$ f = \int (2xy + 3)\,dx = x^2y + 3x + g(y) $$

The "constant" is a function \(g(y)\): anything with only \(y\) in it vanishes when you differentiate by \(x\).

Step 2 — Differentiate with respect to \(y\)
$$ f_y = x^2 + 0 + g'(y) = x^2 + g'(y) $$
Step 3 — Match with \(Q\)
$$ x^2 + g'(y) = x^2 - 4y $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; g'(y) = -4y $$
Step 4 — Integrate to find \(g\)
$$ g(y) = \int -4y\,dy = -2y^2 + C $$
Step 5 — Put it together
\(f(x, y) = x^2y + 3x - 2y^2 + C\)
Check \(f_x = 2xy + 3 = P\) ✓   and   \(f_y = x^2 - 4y = Q\) ✓. (Any value of \(C\) works; \(C = 0\) is the simplest answer.)
