Q1. Rotate and Identify a Conic Conics

Given \(9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2\) \(- 30x - 40y = 0\): (a) find the angle of rotation that eliminates the \(xy\)-term; (b) write the transformation equations; (c) transform the equation; (d) translate to standard form; (e) identify the conic; (f) find its vertex and other properties.
Formulas you need
$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B} $$
$$ \cos\theta = \sqrt{\tfrac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}}, \quad \sin\theta = \sqrt{\tfrac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}} $$
$$ x = X\cos\theta - Y\sin\theta $$
$$ y = X\sin\theta + Y\cos\theta $$
Classify with \(B^2 - 4AC\): negative → ellipse, zero → parabola, positive → hyperbola.
The idea The \(xy\)-term means the curve is tilted. We spin the axes by an angle \(\theta\) until the new \(X\)- and \(Y\)-axes line up with the curve — then the \(xy\)-term disappears and we can read the shape like a normal conic. (Background: Conics notes, Part 8.)

(a) Angle of rotation

$$ A = 9,\quad B = 24,\quad C = 16 $$
$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{9 - 16}{24} = -\frac{7}{24} $$

Draw a right triangle for \(2\theta\): adjacent \(-7\), opposite \(24\), hypotenuse \(\sqrt{49 + 576} = 25\). So \(\cos 2\theta = -\tfrac{7}{25}\) (negative, so \(2\theta\) is between \(90^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\)).

$$ \cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 - 7/25}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{25}} = \frac35 $$
$$ \sin\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 7/25}{2}} = \frac45 $$
\(\theta = \tan^{-1}\tfrac43 \approx 53.13^\circ\), with \(\cos\theta = \tfrac35\), \(\sin\theta = \tfrac45\)

(b) Transformation equations

\(x = \dfrac{3X - 4Y}{5}\),    \(y = \dfrac{4X + 3Y}{5}\)

(c) Transform the equation

Spot the shortcut: the quadratic part is a perfect square, \(9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2 = (3x + 4y)^2\), and the linear part is \(-10(3x + 4y)\). So everything depends on the single combination \(3x + 4y\). Substitute:

$$ 3x + 4y = \frac{9X - 12Y + 16X + 12Y}{5} = 5X $$
$$ (5X)^2 - 10(5X) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 25X^2 - 50X = 0 $$

Check with the coefficient formulas: \(A' = 9\cdot\tfrac{9}{25} + 24\cdot\tfrac{12}{25} + 16\cdot\tfrac{16}{25} = 25\), \(C' = 0\), \(D' = -30\cdot\tfrac35 - 40\cdot\tfrac45 = -50\), \(E' = 30\cdot\tfrac45 - 40\cdot\tfrac35 = 0\). ✓

\(25X^2 - 50X = 0\)

(d) Translate to standard form

$$ X^2 - 2X = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (X - 1)^2 = 1 $$

Shift the origin to \(X = 1\): let \(X' = X - 1\), \(Y' = Y\).

\(X'^2 = 1\)

(e) Identify the conic

The discriminant \(B^2 - 4AC = 576 - 576 = 0\) says "parabola type". But for a true parabola we'd need a \(Y\) term (like \(X^2 = 4aY\)) — and there isn't one. \(X'^2 = 1\) just means \(X' = \pm 1\):

A degenerate parabola: a pair of parallel lines, \(X = 0\) and \(X = 2\) — in \(xy\)-coordinates, \(3x + 4y = 0\) and \(3x + 4y = 10\).

(f) Geometric properties

  • No vertex or focus — those only exist for a genuine parabola.
  • The lines are parallel with slope \(-\tfrac34\) and are \(2\) units apart (\(X = 0\) to \(X = 2\); or \(\tfrac{|10 - 0|}{\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2}} = 2\)).
  • Their line of symmetry is the midline \(X = 1\), i.e. \(3x + 4y = 5\).
  • Quick check by factoring: \((3x + 4y)(3x + 4y - 10) = 0\).
This is very likely a typo on the sheet — the question expects a vertex. A small sign change gives a genuine parabola. Open the box below for a full worked version, so you can see what part (f) is asking for.
Practice variant: \(9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2 - 80x + 60y = 0\) (a genuine parabola)

Same quadratic part, so the same rotation (\(\cos\theta = \tfrac35\), \(\sin\theta = \tfrac45\)) and still \(25X^2\). The linear part:

$$ -80x + 60y $$
$$ = \frac{-80(3X - 4Y) + 60(4X + 3Y)}{5} $$
$$ = 100Y $$
$$ 25X^2 + 100Y = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; X^2 = -4Y $$

Compare with \(X^2 = -4aY\): \(a = 1\), opening in the negative \(Y\) direction.

  • Vertex: \((X,Y) = (0,0)\) → \((x,y) = (0,0)\)
  • Focus: \((X,Y) = (0,-1)\) → \(x = \tfrac{0 + 4}{5} = 0.8\), \(y = \tfrac{0 - 3}{5} = -0.6\)
  • Directrix: \(Y = 1\). Since \(Y = \tfrac{-4x + 3y}{5}\), this is \(-4x + 3y = 5\)
  • Axis of symmetry: \(X = 0\), i.e. \(3x + 4y = 0\); latus rectum length \(4a = 4\)

Q2. A Rotated Ellipse Conics

\(13x^2 + 10xy + 13y^2 = 72\). (a) Find the angle of rotation; (b) the transformed equation; (c) identify the conic; (d) find the semi-major and semi-minor axes.
Formula you need
$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B} $$
$$ A = C \;\Rightarrow\; \theta = 45^\circ $$
The idea \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) have the same coefficient, so the shape is symmetric about the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\). Rotating by \(45^\circ\) lines the axes up with those diagonals.

(a) Angle

\(\cot 2\theta = \tfrac{13 - 13}{10} = 0\) \(\Rightarrow 2\theta = 90^\circ\) \(\Rightarrow \theta = 45^\circ\)

(b) Transformed equation

With \(\theta = 45^\circ\): \(x = \tfrac{X - Y}{\sqrt2}\), \(y = \tfrac{X + Y}{\sqrt2}\). Two neat facts make this quick:

$$ x^2 + y^2 = X^2 + Y^2, \qquad xy = \frac{X^2 - Y^2}{2} $$

Rewrite the equation as \(13(x^2 + y^2) + 10xy = 72\):

$$ 13(X^2 + Y^2) + 5(X^2 - Y^2) = 72 $$
$$ 18X^2 + 8Y^2 = 72 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{X^2}{4} + \frac{Y^2}{9} = 1 $$
\(\dfrac{X^2}{4} + \dfrac{Y^2}{9} = 1\)

(c) Identify

Ellipse (\(B^2 - 4AC = 100 - 676 < 0\), and both squared terms are positive)

(d) Semi-axes

Semi-major axis \(a = 3\) (along \(Y\)); semi-minor axis \(b = 2\) (along \(X\))
What it means The ellipse is 6 units long and 4 units wide, tilted \(45^\circ\). Bonus: \(c = \sqrt{9 - 4} = \sqrt5\), so the foci sit \(\sqrt5\) from the centre along the \(Y\)-axis, and \(e = \tfrac{\sqrt5}{3} \approx 0.745\).

Q3. One Point, Three Descriptions Coordinates

A point has polar coordinates \((r, \theta) = \left(6, \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)\) and \(z = 4\). Find (a) its Cartesian coordinates; (b) cylindrical coordinates; (c) distance from the \(z\)-axis; (d) distance from the origin.
Formulas you need
$$ x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad z = z $$
Distance from \(z\)-axis \(= r\);  distance from origin \(= \rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} = \sqrt{r^2 + z^2}\).
The idea Polar \((r,\theta)\) plus a height \(z\) is cylindrical coordinates. \(r\) is how far you are from the vertical axis; adding the height with Pythagoras gives how far you are from the origin.

(a) Cartesian

$$ x = 6\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{3} = 6\left(-\tfrac12\right) = -3 $$
$$ y = 6\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3} = 6\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 3\sqrt3 $$
\((x, y, z) = (-3,\ 3\sqrt3,\ 4)\) \(\approx (-3,\ 5.196,\ 4)\)

(b) Cylindrical

\((r, \theta, z) = \left(6,\ \tfrac{2\pi}{3},\ 4\right)\) — exactly what we were given

(c) Distance from the z-axis

\(r = 6\)

(d) Distance from the origin

$$ \sqrt{r^2 + z^2} = \sqrt{36 + 16} = \sqrt{52} = 2\sqrt{13} $$
\(2\sqrt{13} \approx 7.21\)
Check\(\sqrt{(-3)^2 + (3\sqrt3)^2 + 4^2}\) \(= \sqrt{9 + 27 + 16} = \sqrt{52}\). ✓

Q4. Flux Through a Paraboloid; Divergence Theorem Unit 7

(a) Find the flux of \(\mathbf{F} = x\mathbf{i} - y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}\) across \(\sigma\): the part of \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\) above the \(xy\)-plane, oriented up.
(b) Use the divergence theorem to find the outward flux of \(\mathbf{F} = 8x^3\mathbf{i} + 8y^3\mathbf{j} + 8z^3\mathbf{k}\) across the surface of the region enclosed by \(z = \sqrt{25 - x^2 - y^2}\) and \(z = 0\).

(a) Flux through an open surface

Formula you need For \(z = g(x,y)\), oriented up, over the shadow \(R\):
$$ \iint_\sigma \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS $$
$$ = \iint_R \left(-P g_x - Q g_y + R\right) dA $$
The idea The paraboloid is an upside-down bowl. We want how much of the field flows up and out through its skin. The formula flattens the problem onto the bowl's shadow — a disc — where polar coordinates make it easy. (Background: Unit 7 notes, Part 4.)
Step 1 — Pieces of the formula
$$ g = 4 - x^2 - y^2, \quad g_x = -2x, \quad g_y = -2y $$
$$ P = x, \quad Q = -y, \quad R = z = 4 - x^2 - y^2 $$
Step 2 — The integrand
$$ -x(-2x) - (-y)(-2y) + (4 - x^2 - y^2) $$
$$ = 2x^2 - 2y^2 + 4 - x^2 - y^2 = x^2 - 3y^2 + 4 $$
Step 3 — Polar over the disc \(r \le 2\)
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 \left(r^2\cos^2\theta - 3r^2\sin^2\theta + 4\right) r\,dr\,d\theta $$

Split it up, using \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi\) and \(\int_0^2 r^3\,dr = 4\):

$$ 4\pi - 3(4\pi) + 4\cdot\left(\pi\cdot 2^2\right) = 4\pi - 12\pi + 16\pi $$
Flux \(= 8\pi \approx 25.13\)
What it means Positive: overall, more of the field flows up through the cap than down into it. The \(+4\) part (from \(z\)) dominates; the \(x\) and \(y\) parts partly cancel because \(\mathbf{F}\) pushes out in \(x\) but in in \(y\).

(b) Divergence theorem over a hemisphere

Formula you need
$$ \oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_G \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\;dV $$
$$ dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
The idea The surface (dome + flat base) is closed, so instead of two hard surface integrals we do one triple integral of the divergence. The solid is half a ball → spherical coordinates.
Step 1 — Divergence
$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 24x^2 + 24y^2 + 24z^2 = 24\rho^2 $$
Step 2 — Spherical limits for the upper half-ball
$$ 0 \le \rho \le 5, \qquad 0 \le \phi \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi $$
Step 3 — Integrate (constant limits, so it splits into three pieces)
$$ \iiint 24\rho^2\cdot\rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
$$ = 24\left[\frac{\rho^5}{5}\right]_0^5 \cdot \Big[-\cos\phi\Big]_0^{\pi/2} \cdot 2\pi $$
$$ = 24 \cdot 625 \cdot 1 \cdot 2\pi = 30000\pi $$
Outward flux \(= 30000\pi \approx 94{,}248\)
Don't forget the Jacobian \(\rho^2\sin\phi\): the integrand becomes \(24\rho^4\sin\phi\), not \(24\rho^2\).

Q5. A Triple Integral That Vanishes by Symmetry Coordinates

Use cylindrical coordinates to evaluate
$$ \int_{-4}^{4}\int_{-\sqrt{16-y^2}}^{\sqrt{16-y^2}}\int_0^{16-4x^2-4y^2} 2x\;dz\,dx\,dy $$
Formula you need
$$ x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta $$
$$ dz\,dx\,dy = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
The idea The \(x, y\) limits describe a full disc centred at the origin. The integrand \(2x\) is positive on the right half and exactly as negative on the left half — so the total must be zero.
Step 1 — Translate the limits

\(x\) runs from \(-\sqrt{16 - y^2}\) to \(\sqrt{16 - y^2}\) and \(y\) from \(-4\) to \(4\): that's the disc \(x^2 + y^2 \le 16\), so \(0 \le r \le 4\), \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\). The top limit becomes \(z = 16 - 4r^2\).

Step 2 — Set up in cylindrical
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^4\!\!\int_0^{16 - 4r^2} 2r\cos\theta \cdot r\;dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
Step 3 — The θ-integral kills it
$$ = \left(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta\right)\cdot\left(\int_0^4 2r^2(16 - 4r^2)\,dr\right) $$
$$ = 0 \cdot (\ldots) $$
\(0\)
The upper limit \(16 - 4r^2\) is negative when \(r > 2\), so as written the integral partly runs "downwards". That doesn't change the answer — the \(\theta\)-integral is zero whatever the radial part is — but if the intended disc was \(r \le 2\) (where \(z = 16 - 4r^2\) meets \(z = 0\)), the answer is still 0.

Q6. Potential Function; Volume of a Hemisphere Unit 6

(a) \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (y + 3x^2y + 2xy)\mathbf{i}\) \(+ (x + x^3 + x^2)\mathbf{j}\). (i) Show \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative on the \(xy\)-plane. (ii) Find \(\phi(x,y)\). (iii) Find \(\int_{(1,2)}^{(3,4)}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\).
(b) Use a triple integral to find the volume bounded by \(z = \sqrt{25 - x^2 - y^2}\) and \(z = 0\).

(a)(i) Is it conservative? — the slide method

Formula you need
$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{conservative} $$
$$ \int_A^B \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(B) - \phi(A) $$
$$ P = y + 3x^2y + 2xy, \qquad Q = x + x^3 + x^2 $$
$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 1 + 3x^2 + 2x $$
$$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 1 + 3x^2 + 2x $$
Equal everywhere in the plane → \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative

(a)(ii) Find φ

$$ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} = y + 3x^2y + 2xy $$
$$ \phi = xy + x^3y + x^2y + g(y) $$
$$ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} = x + x^3 + x^2 + g'(y) $$

This must equal \(Q = x + x^3 + x^2\) — everything matches already, so \(g'(y) = 0\) and \(g\) is a constant.

\(\phi = xy + x^3y + x^2y + C\)

(a)(iii) The line integral

The idea For a conservative field the path doesn't matter — only where you start and finish. Like height on a hill: the climb from A to B is "height at B minus height at A", whichever route you take.
$$ \phi(3,4) = 12 + 108 + 36 = 156 $$
$$ \phi(1,2) = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 $$
\(\displaystyle\int_{(1,2)}^{(3,4)}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 156 - 6 = 150\)

(b) Volume of the hemisphere

Formula you need
$$ V = \iiint_G 1\,dV $$
$$ = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{5} \rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
$$ V = \left[\frac{\rho^3}{3}\right]_0^5 \cdot \Big[-\cos\phi\Big]_0^{\pi/2} \cdot 2\pi $$
$$ = \frac{125}{3}\cdot 1\cdot 2\pi $$
\(V = \dfrac{250\pi}{3} \approx 261.8\)
CheckHalf of \(\tfrac43\pi(5)^3 = \tfrac{500\pi}{3}\) is \(\tfrac{250\pi}{3}\). ✓

Q7. Divergence Theorem; Surface Integral on a Plane Unit 7

(a) Use the divergence theorem to find the outward flux of \(\mathbf{F} = 2x\mathbf{i} + 2y\mathbf{j} + 3z\mathbf{k}\) across the surface of the region enclosed by \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\) and \(z = 0\).
(b) Evaluate \(\iint_\sigma (x + y)\,dS\), where \(\sigma\) is the part of the plane \(2x + 3y + z = 6\) in the first octant.

(a) Constant divergence → flux = 7 × volume

The idea The divergence works out to a constant, 7. That means "7 units produced per unit volume", so the outward flux is just 7 times the volume of the solid.
$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 2 + 2 + 3 = 7 $$
Volume of the solid (cylindrical: \(0 \le z \le 4 - r^2\), \(r \le 2\))
$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 (4 - r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = 2\pi\left[2r^2 - \frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = 2\pi(8 - 4) = 8\pi $$
Outward flux \(= 7 \times 8\pi = 56\pi \approx 175.9\)

(b) \(\iint (x + y)\,dS\) on a plane

Formula you need
$$ \iint_\sigma f\,dS = \iint_R f\,\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\;dA $$
The idea A tilted plane is bigger than its shadow by a constant factor. Find that factor, then integrate \(x + y\) over the shadow triangle. (\(x + y\) contains no \(z\), so nothing needs substituting.)
Step 1 — Stretch factor
$$ z = 6 - 2x - 3y, \quad g_x = -2, \quad g_y = -3 $$
$$ \Rightarrow\; \sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 1} = \sqrt{14} $$
Step 2 — The shadow (set \(z = 0\))

\(2x + 3y = 6\) meets the axes at \((3, 0)\) and \((0, 2)\). With \(x, y \ge 0\), the shadow is the triangle below:

Step 3 — Integrate
$$ \int_0^3\!\!\int_0^{(6-2x)/3} (x + y)\,dy\,dx $$
$$ = \int_0^3 \left(2 + \tfrac23 x - \tfrac49 x^2\right) dx $$
$$ = \left[2x + \frac{x^2}{3} - \frac{4x^3}{27}\right]_0^3 = 6 + 3 - 4 = 5 $$
\(\iint_\sigma (x + y)\,dS = 5\sqrt{14} \approx 18.71\)
Check The triangle has area 3 and its centroid is \((1, \tfrac23)\), so the average of \(x + y\) is \(\tfrac53\) and \(\iint_R (x + y)\,dA = 3 \times \tfrac53 = 5\). ✓

Q8. Convert an Equation; Green's Theorem Mixed

Express \(x + y^2 = 2z\) in cylindrical and spherical coordinates. Using Green's theorem, find \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\), where \(\mathbf{F} = 2\mathbf{i} - x\mathbf{j}\) and \(C\) is the boundary of the region in the first quadrant enclosed by the coordinate axes and the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\).

Part 1 — Converting the equation

Formulas you need Cylindrical: \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\), \(z = z\).   Spherical: \(x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta\), \(y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta\), \(z = \rho\cos\phi\).
The ideaJust substitute — every \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) gets replaced by its formula.
Cylindrical: \(r\cos\theta + r^2\sin^2\theta = 2z\)
$$ \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta + \rho^2\sin^2\phi\sin^2\theta = 2\rho\cos\phi $$
Spherical (dividing by \(\rho \ne 0\)): \(\sin\phi\cos\theta + \rho\sin^2\phi\sin^2\theta\) \(= 2\cos\phi\)

Part 2 — Green's theorem on a quarter disc

Formula you need
$$ \oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_R \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA $$
The idea \(Q_x - P_y\) comes out as a constant, so the answer is that constant times the area of the quarter disc.
$$ P = 2, \quad Q = -x $$
$$ \Rightarrow \; \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = -1 - 0 = -1 $$
$$ \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_R (-1)\,dA = -\text{area} = -\frac{\pi}{4} $$
\(-\dfrac{\pi}{4} \approx -0.785\) (counterclockwise orientation)
What it meansThe field \(-x\mathbf{j}\) spins clockwise, so its circulation counterclockwise is negative.

Q9. Flux Through a Paraboloid; Divergence Theorem Unit 7

(a) Flux of \(\mathbf{F} = x\mathbf{i} - y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}\) across the part of \(z = 9 - x^2 - y^2\) above the \(xy\)-plane, oriented up.
(b) Outward flux of \(\mathbf{F} = 4x^3\mathbf{i} + 4y^3\mathbf{j} + 4z^3\mathbf{k}\) across the surface of the region enclosed by \(z = \sqrt{16 - x^2 - y^2}\) and \(z = 0\).
The idea This is Q4 with different numbers — same field in (a), same kind of solid in (b). Follow the same steps.

(a) Same as Q4(a), with 9 instead of 4

\(g_x = -2x\), \(g_y = -2y\), so the integrand is \(x^2 - 3y^2 + 9\), over the disc \(r \le 3\):

$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^3 \left(r^2\cos^2\theta - 3r^2\sin^2\theta + 9\right) r\,dr\,d\theta $$

\(\int_0^3 r^3\,dr = \tfrac{81}{4}\), and the area of the disc is \(9\pi\):

$$ \frac{81}{4}(\pi - 3\pi) + 9(9\pi) = -\frac{81\pi}{2} + 81\pi $$
Flux \(= \dfrac{81\pi}{2} \approx 127.2\)
Pattern For this field and \(z = k - x^2 - y^2\), the flux is always \(\tfrac{\pi k^2}{2}\): \(k = 4\) gives \(8\pi\) (Q4a), \(k = 9\) gives \(\tfrac{81\pi}{2}\). ✓

(b) Same as Q4(b), radius 4

$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 12(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) = 12\rho^2 $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^4 12\rho^4\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
$$ = 12\cdot\frac{4^5}{5}\cdot 1\cdot 2\pi $$
Outward flux \(= \dfrac{24576\pi}{5} \approx 15{,}442\)

Q10. Cylindrical Integral; Ice-Cream Cone Volume Coordinates

(a) Use cylindrical coordinates to evaluate the same integral as Q5 but with integrand \(8y\).
(b) Use a triple integral to find the volume of the solid enclosed by the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\) and the cone \(z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\).

(a) Symmetry again

Same disc, integrand \(8y = 8r\sin\theta\). This time the top half is positive and the bottom half negative (right-hand panel of the Q5 picture):

$$ \left(\int_0^{2\pi}\sin\theta\,d\theta\right)\cdot(\ldots) = 0 $$
\(0\)

(b) Volume between a cone and a sphere

Formula you need
$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\phi_0}\!\!\int_0^{a} \rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
The idea This solid looks like an ice-cream cone: a cone for the bottom and a scoop of sphere on top. In spherical coordinates both boundaries are simple: the sphere is \(\rho = 2\), and the cone \(z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) makes a \(45^\circ\) angle with the \(z\)-axis, so it's \(\phi = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\).
Why the cone is φ = π/4

On the cone, \(z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = r\). In spherical, \(z = \rho\cos\phi\) and \(r = \rho\sin\phi\), so \(\cos\phi = \sin\phi\), i.e. \(\phi = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\).

Integrate
$$ V = \left[\frac{\rho^3}{3}\right]_0^2\cdot\Big[-\cos\phi\Big]_0^{\pi/4}\cdot 2\pi $$
$$ = \frac83\cdot\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\cdot 2\pi $$
\(V = \dfrac{8\pi}{3}\left(2 - \sqrt2\right) \approx 4.91\)
Sanity checkThe whole ball has volume \(\tfrac{32\pi}{3} \approx 33.5\). Our cone only takes a narrow wedge (\(\phi\) up to \(45^\circ\) instead of \(180^\circ\)), so about 15% of that is reasonable.

Q11. Potential Function; Green's Theorem on a Circle Unit 6–7

(a) \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (2xy^2 + 3x^2y^3)\mathbf{i}\) \(+ (2yx^2 + 3y^2x^3)\mathbf{j}\). (i) Show \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative; (ii) find \(\phi\); (iii) find \(\int_{(0,0)}^{(1,1)}\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\).
(b) Using Green's theorem, find \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for \(\mathbf{F} = (25e^{3x} - y^3)\mathbf{i} + (5y^3 + x^3)\mathbf{j}\), \(C\): \(x = 2\cos t\), \(y = 2\sin t\), \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\).

(a) Conservative field — the slide method

$$ P = 2xy^2 + 3x^2y^3, \qquad Q = 2yx^2 + 3y^2x^3 $$
$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 4xy + 9x^2y^2, \qquad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 4xy + 9x^2y^2 $$
(i) Equal → conservative
$$ \phi = \int (2xy^2 + 3x^2y^3)\,dx $$
$$ = x^2y^2 + x^3y^3 + g(y) $$
$$ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} = 2x^2y + 3x^3y^2 + g'(y) $$
$$ = Q \;\Rightarrow\; g'(y) = 0 $$
(ii) \(\phi = x^2y^2 + x^3y^3 + C\)
(iii) \(\phi(1,1) - \phi(0,0)\) \(= (1 + 1) - 0 = 2\)

(b) Green's theorem on a circle

The idea The \(25e^{3x}\) term looks scary, but Green's theorem only uses \(\partial P/\partial y\) — and \(25e^{3x}\) has no \(y\), so it disappears. What's left is a nice \(x^2 + y^2\), perfect for polar coordinates.
$$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 3x^2, \qquad \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = -3y^2 $$
$$ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 3x^2 + 3y^2 = 3r^2 $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 3r^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = 3\cdot\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2\cdot 2\pi = 3\cdot 4\cdot 2\pi $$
\(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 24\pi \approx 75.4\)
CheckComputing the line integral directly around the circle (numerically) also gives \(75.40\). ✓

Q12. Hemisphere Volume; Surface Integral on a Plane Mixed

(a) Use spherical coordinates to find the volume of the solid bounded above by \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 36\) and below by the \(xy\)-plane.
(b) Evaluate \(\iint_\sigma yz\,dS\), where \(\sigma\) is the part of the plane \(x - 2y + z = 4\) in the first octant.

(a) Upper half-ball, radius 6

$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^6 \rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
$$ = \frac{216}{3}\cdot 1\cdot 2\pi $$
\(V = 144\pi \approx 452.4\)

(b) The plane as printed doesn't give a finite surface

Problem with the question: on \(x - 2y + z = 4\), \(z = 4 - x + 2y\). In the first octant we need \(x, y, z \ge 0\) — but for any \(y \ge 0\), every \(x\) from 0 up to \(4 + 2y\) works. As \(y\) grows, the allowed region keeps growing forever, so the surface is infinite and the integral doesn't converge. The likely intended plane is \(x + 2y + z = 4\), which cuts off a finite triangle. Solved below for that plane.
Formula you need
$$ \iint_\sigma f\,dS $$
$$ = \iint_R f\big(x, y, g(x,y)\big)\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\;dA $$
Step 1 — Surface and stretch factor
$$ z = 4 - x - 2y, \quad g_x = -1, \quad g_y = -2 $$
$$ \Rightarrow\; \sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt6 $$
Step 2 — Substitute z into the integrand
$$ yz = y(4 - x - 2y) $$
Step 3 — The shadow triangle (set \(z = 0\): \(x + 2y = 4\)) Step 4 — Integrate (x first)

With \(w = 4 - 2y\): \(\int_0^{w} y(w - x)\,dx = y\cdot\tfrac{w^2}{2}\).

$$ \int_0^2 y\,\frac{(4 - 2y)^2}{2}\,dy = 2\int_0^2 y(2 - y)^2\,dy $$
$$ = 2\left[2y^2 - \frac{4y^3}{3} + \frac{y^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = 2\cdot\frac43 = \frac83 $$
For \(x + 2y + z = 4\): \(\iint_\sigma yz\,dS = \dfrac{8\sqrt6}{3} \approx 6.53\)

(a) \(\sigma\): the part of \(z = 25 - x^2 - y^2\) above the \(xy\)-plane, oriented up. Find the flux of \(\mathbf{F} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}\) across \(\sigma\).
(b) Use the divergence theorem to find the outward flux of \(\mathbf{F} = x^3\mathbf{i} + y^3\mathbf{j} + z^2\mathbf{k}\) across the surface of the region enclosed by \(x^2 + y^2 = 16\), \(z = 2\) and \(z = 4\).

(a) Flux up through the cap

The ideaSame method as Q4(a); this time \(\mathbf{F}\) points straight out from the origin, so every part of the integrand is positive.
$$ -P g_x - Q g_y + R $$
$$ = x(2x) + y(2y) + (25 - x^2 - y^2) $$
$$ = x^2 + y^2 + 25 $$

Q14. Scalar Line Integral; Rate of Temperature Change Unit 6

Evaluate \(\int_C (x + y)\,ds\), \(C\) the straight segment from \(A(0,0)\) to \(B(1,1)\). Also: \(T(x,y,z) = x^2 + 2y^2 + 3z^2\); at \(P(1,-1,2)\), find the rate at which temperature increases in the direction from \(P\) toward \(Q(3,2,3)\).

Part 1 — Line integral of a scalar field

Formula you need
$$ \int_C f\,ds $$
$$ = \int_a^b f\big(x(t), y(t)\big)\sqrt{x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2}\,dt $$
The ideaWalk along the segment and add up \(x + y\) times each tiny length \(ds\). A straight line has constant speed, so \(ds\) is a constant times \(dt\).
$$ x = t,\quad y = t,\quad 0 \le t \le 1 $$
$$ \Rightarrow \; ds = \sqrt{1 + 1}\,dt = \sqrt2\,dt $$
$$ \int_0^1 (t + t)\sqrt2\,dt = 2\sqrt2\cdot\frac12 = \sqrt2 $$
\(\int_C (x + y)\,ds = \sqrt2 \approx 1.414\)

Part 2 — Directional derivative

Formula you need
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}T = \nabla T\cdot\mathbf{u}, \qquad \mathbf{u} = \frac{\overrightarrow{PQ}}{|\overrightarrow{PQ}|} $$
$$ \nabla T = \langle 2x, 4y, 6z\rangle $$
$$ \nabla T(1,-1,2) = \langle 2, -4, 12\rangle $$
$$ \overrightarrow{PQ} = \langle 2, 3, 1\rangle, \qquad |\overrightarrow{PQ}| = \sqrt{14} $$
$$ D_{\mathbf{u}}T = \frac{4 - 12 + 12}{\sqrt{14}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{14}} $$
\(\dfrac{4}{\sqrt{14}} \approx 1.069\)

Q16. Gradient of a Physical Quantity Unit 6

\(f(x,y,z) = x^2y + yz^2 + 3x\). (a) Find \(\nabla f\). (b) Find it at \(P(1,2,-1)\). (c) Explain what the direction of \(\nabla f\) represents physically.
Formula you need
$$ \nabla f = \left\langle \frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial y},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \right\rangle $$
The ideaEach partial derivative is the slope along one axis (treat the other letters as constants). The gradient bundles them into one arrow.

(a)

$$ f_x = 2xy + 3 $$
$$ f_y = x^2 + z^2 $$
$$ f_z = 2yz $$
\(\nabla f = (2xy + 3)\mathbf{i}\) \(+ (x^2 + z^2)\mathbf{j} + 2yz\,\mathbf{k}\)

(b)

$$ \nabla f(1,2,-1) $$
$$ = (4 + 3)\mathbf{i} + (1 + 1)\mathbf{j} + (2\cdot 2\cdot(-1))\mathbf{k} $$
\(\nabla f(1,2,-1) = 7\mathbf{i} + 2\mathbf{j} - 4\mathbf{k}\)

(c) Physical meaning

  • The direction \(\langle 7, 2, -4\rangle\) is where the quantity \(f\) increases fastest from \(P\) — e.g. if \(f\) were temperature, the way to walk to warm up quickest.
  • The rate in that direction is \(|\nabla f| = \sqrt{49 + 4 + 16}\) \(= \sqrt{69} \approx 8.31\) per unit distance.
  • It is perpendicular to the level surface \(f = f(P) = 7\) through \(P\) — moving along that surface keeps \(f\) constant.
  • \(-\nabla f\) is the direction of fastest decrease (heat flows this way).
