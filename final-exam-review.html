Q1. Rotate and Identify a Conic Conics

Tap to enlarge
🧭 Lost on this one? Q1 Explained goes through every step slowly, with pictures and a slider that turns the axes for you.
Formulas you need
$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B} $$
$$ \cos\theta = \sqrt{\tfrac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}}, \quad \sin\theta = \sqrt{\tfrac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}} $$
$$ x = X\cos\theta - Y\sin\theta $$
$$ y = X\sin\theta + Y\cos\theta $$
Classify with \(B^2 - 4AC\): negative → ellipse, zero → parabola, positive → hyperbola.
The idea The \(xy\)-term means the curve is tilted. We spin the axes by an angle \(\theta\) until the new \(X\)- and \(Y\)-axes line up with the curve — then the \(xy\)-term disappears and we can read the shape like a normal conic. (Background: Conics notes, Part 8.)

(a) Angle of rotation

$$ A = 9,\quad B = 24,\quad C = 16 $$
$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{9 - 16}{24} = -\frac{7}{24} $$

Draw a right triangle for \(2\theta\): adjacent \(-7\), opposite \(24\), hypotenuse \(\sqrt{49 + 576} = 25\). So \(\cos 2\theta = -\tfrac{7}{25}\) (negative, so \(2\theta\) is between \(90^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\)).

$$ \cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 - 7/25}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{25}} = \frac35 $$
$$ \sin\theta = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 7/25}{2}} = \frac45 $$
\(\theta = \tan^{-1}\tfrac43 \approx 53.13^\circ\), with \(\cos\theta = \tfrac35\), \(\sin\theta = \tfrac45\)

(b) Transformation equations

\(x = \dfrac{3X - 4Y}{5}\),    \(y = \dfrac{4X + 3Y}{5}\)

(c) Transform the equation

Spot the shortcut: the quadratic part is a perfect square, \(9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2 = (3x + 4y)^2\), and the linear part is \(-10(3x + 4y)\). So everything depends on the single combination \(3x + 4y\). Substitute:

$$ 3x + 4y = \frac{9X - 12Y + 16X + 12Y}{5} = 5X $$
$$ (5X)^2 - 10(5X) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 25X^2 - 50X = 0 $$

Check with the coefficient formulas: \(A' = 9\cdot\tfrac{9}{25} + 24\cdot\tfrac{12}{25} + 16\cdot\tfrac{16}{25} = 25\), \(C' = 0\), \(D' = -30\cdot\tfrac35 - 40\cdot\tfrac45 = -50\), \(E' = 30\cdot\tfrac45 - 40\cdot\tfrac35 = 0\). ✓

\(25X^2 - 50X = 0\)

(d) Translate to standard form

$$ X^2 - 2X = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (X - 1)^2 = 1 $$

Shift the origin to \(X = 1\): let \(X' = X - 1\), \(Y' = Y\).

\(X'^2 = 1\)

(e) Identify the conic

The discriminant \(B^2 - 4AC = 576 - 576 = 0\) says "parabola type". But for a true parabola we'd need a \(Y\) term (like \(X^2 = 4aY\)) — and there isn't one. \(X'^2 = 1\) just means \(X' = \pm 1\):

A degenerate parabola: a pair of parallel lines, \(X = 0\) and \(X = 2\) — in \(xy\)-coordinates, \(3x + 4y = 0\) and \(3x + 4y = 10\).

(f) Geometric properties

  • No vertex or focus — those only exist for a genuine parabola.
  • The lines are parallel with slope \(-\tfrac34\) and are \(2\) units apart (\(X = 0\) to \(X = 2\); or \(\tfrac{|10 - 0|}{\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2}} = 2\)).
  • Their line of symmetry is the midline \(X = 1\), i.e. \(3x + 4y = 5\).
  • Quick check by factoring: \((3x + 4y)(3x + 4y - 10) = 0\).
This is very likely a typo on the sheet — the question expects a vertex. A small sign change gives a genuine parabola. Open the box below for a full worked version, so you can see what part (f) is asking for.
Practice variant: \(9x^2 + 24xy + 16y^2 - 80x + 60y = 0\) (a genuine parabola)

Same quadratic part, so the same rotation (\(\cos\theta = \tfrac35\), \(\sin\theta = \tfrac45\)) and still \(25X^2\). The linear part:

$$ -80x + 60y $$
$$ = \frac{-80(3X - 4Y) + 60(4X + 3Y)}{5} $$
$$ = 100Y $$
$$ 25X^2 + 100Y = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; X^2 = -4Y $$

Compare with \(X^2 = -4aY\): \(a = 1\), opening in the negative \(Y\) direction.

  • Vertex: \((X,Y) = (0,0)\) → \((x,y) = (0,0)\)
  • Focus: \((X,Y) = (0,-1)\) → \(x = \tfrac{0 + 4}{5} = 0.8\), \(y = \tfrac{0 - 3}{5} = -0.6\)
  • Directrix: \(Y = 1\). Since \(Y = \tfrac{-4x + 3y}{5}\), this is \(-4x + 3y = 5\)
  • Axis of symmetry: \(X = 0\), i.e. \(3x + 4y = 0\); latus rectum length \(4a = 4\)

Q2. A Rotated Ellipse Conics

Tap to enlarge
Formula you need
$$ \cot 2\theta = \frac{A - C}{B} $$
$$ A = C \;\Rightarrow\; \theta = 45^\circ $$
The idea \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) have the same coefficient, so the shape is symmetric about the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = -x\). Rotating by \(45^\circ\) lines the axes up with those diagonals.

(a) Angle

\(\cot 2\theta = \tfrac{13 - 13}{10} = 0\) \(\Rightarrow 2\theta = 90^\circ\) \(\Rightarrow \theta = 45^\circ\)

(b) Transformed equation

With \(\theta = 45^\circ\): \(x = \tfrac{X - Y}{\sqrt2}\), \(y = \tfrac{X + Y}{\sqrt2}\). Two neat facts make this quick:

$$ x^2 + y^2 = X^2 + Y^2, \qquad xy = \frac{X^2 - Y^2}{2} $$

Rewrite the equation as \(13(x^2 + y^2) + 10xy = 72\):

$$ 13(X^2 + Y^2) + 5(X^2 - Y^2) = 72 $$
$$ 18X^2 + 8Y^2 = 72 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{X^2}{4} + \frac{Y^2}{9} = 1 $$
\(\dfrac{X^2}{4} + \dfrac{Y^2}{9} = 1\)

(c) Identify

Ellipse (\(B^2 - 4AC = 100 - 676 < 0\), and both squared terms are positive)

(d) Semi-axes

Semi-major axis \(a = 3\) (along \(Y\)); semi-minor axis \(b = 2\) (along \(X\))
What it means The ellipse is 6 units long and 4 units wide, tilted \(45^\circ\). Bonus: \(c = \sqrt{9 - 4} = \sqrt5\), so the foci sit \(\sqrt5\) from the centre along the \(Y\)-axis, and \(e = \tfrac{\sqrt5}{3} \approx 0.745\).

Q4. Flux Through a Paraboloid; Divergence Theorem Unit 7

Tap to enlarge

(a) Flux through an open surface

Formula you need For \(z = g(x,y)\), oriented up, over the shadow \(R\):
$$ \iint_\sigma \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS $$
$$ = \iint_R \left(-P g_x - Q g_y + R\right) dA $$
The idea The paraboloid is an upside-down bowl. We want how much of the field flows up and out through its skin. The formula flattens the problem onto the bowl's shadow — a disc — where polar coordinates make it easy. (Background: Unit 7 notes, Part 4.)
Step 1 — The surface: find \(g\), \(g_x\), \(g_y\)

The surface is written as \(z = g(x, y)\), so \(g\) is everything on the right:

$$ g = 4 - x^2 - y^2 $$

\(g_x\): differentiate with respect to \(x\), treating \(y\) as a constant (so \(4\) and \(y^2\) disappear). \(g_y\): the same with \(x\) as the constant.

$$ g_x = -2x, \qquad g_y = -2y $$
Step 2 — The field: find \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\)

\(P\), \(Q\), \(R\) are the numbers in front of \(\mathbf{i}\), \(\mathbf{j}\), \(\mathbf{k}\) in \(\mathbf{F} = x\,\mathbf{i} - y\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\) — keep the minus sign on \(Q\):

$$ P = x, \qquad Q = -y, \qquad R = z $$

We're on the surface, so \(z\) is not free — it equals \(g\). Replace it:

$$ R = z = 4 - x^2 - y^2 $$
Step 3 — Build the integrand \(-P g_x - Q g_y + R\), one piece at a time
$$ -P\,g_x = -(x)(-2x) = 2x^2 $$

Minus × minus = plus.

$$ -Q\,g_y = -(-y)(-2y) = -2y^2 $$

Careful here: \((-y)(-2y) = +2y^2\), and the minus in front makes it \(-2y^2\).

$$ R = 4 - x^2 - y^2 $$

Add the three pieces and collect like terms (\(x^2\) with \(x^2\), \(y^2\) with \(y^2\)):

$$ 2x^2 - 2y^2 + 4 - x^2 - y^2 $$
$$ = (2 - 1)x^2 + (-2 - 1)y^2 + 4 = x^2 - 3y^2 + 4 $$
So the flux is \(\iint_R (x^2 - 3y^2 + 4)\,dA\) over the shadow disc \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\). Everything from here is just doing that double integral.
Step 4 — Switch to polar (the shadow is a disc)

The shadow is the disc \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\), so use polar coordinates. Three things change:

  • \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\)
  • \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\) — don't forget this extra \(r\)
  • limits: \(r\) from 0 to 2 (the radius), \(\theta\) from 0 to \(2\pi\) (all the way round)
$$ x^2 - 3y^2 + 4 = r^2\cos^2\theta - 3r^2\sin^2\theta + 4 $$
Step 5 — Set up the integral (and multiply in the extra \(r\))
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 \left(r^2\cos^2\theta - 3r^2\sin^2\theta + 4\right) r\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 \left(r^3\cos^2\theta - 3r^3\sin^2\theta + 4r\right) dr\,d\theta $$
Step 6 — Inner integral first: \(dr\) (treat \(\theta\) as a constant)

The \(\cos^2\theta\) and \(\sin^2\theta\) are just numbers while we integrate in \(r\), so they come along for the ride. We need two basic integrals:

$$ \int_0^2 r^3\,dr = \left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = \frac{16}{4} - 0 = 4 $$
$$ \int_0^2 4r\,dr = \Big[2r^2\Big]_0^2 = 8 - 0 = 8 $$

Put them in, term by term:

$$ \int_0^2 \left(r^3\cos^2\theta - 3r^3\sin^2\theta + 4r\right) dr $$
$$ = 4\cos^2\theta - 3(4)\sin^2\theta + 8 $$
$$ = 4\cos^2\theta - 12\sin^2\theta + 8 $$
Step 7 — Outer integral: \(d\theta\) from 0 to \(2\pi\)
$$ \int_0^{2\pi} \left(4\cos^2\theta - 12\sin^2\theta + 8\right) d\theta $$

Again term by term. The \(\cos^2\theta\) and \(\sin^2\theta\) integrals use the half-angle identities:

$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}\,d\theta $$
$$ = \left[\frac{\theta}{2} + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\right]_0^{2\pi} = \pi + 0 - 0 = \pi $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta = \left[\frac{\theta}{2} - \frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\right]_0^{2\pi} = \pi $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi} 8\,d\theta = 8 \cdot 2\pi = 16\pi $$
Shortcut worth remembering: over a full turn, \(\cos^2\theta\) and \(\sin^2\theta\) each average \(\tfrac12\), so each integrates to \(\tfrac12 \times 2\pi = \pi\).
Step 8 — Add the three pieces
$$ 4(\pi) - 12(\pi) + 16\pi = 4\pi - 12\pi + 16\pi = 8\pi $$
Flux \(= 8\pi \approx 25.13\)
What it means Positive: overall, more of the field flows up through the cap than down into it. The \(+4\) part (from \(z\)) dominates; the \(x\) and \(y\) parts partly cancel because \(\mathbf{F}\) pushes out in \(x\) but in in \(y\).

(b) Divergence theorem over a hemisphere

Formula you need
$$ \oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_G \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\;dV $$
$$ dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
The idea The surface (dome + flat base) is closed, so instead of two hard surface integrals we do one triple integral of the divergence. The solid is half a ball → spherical coordinates.
Step 1 — Why the divergence theorem?

The region is closed in: a curved dome on top (\(z = \sqrt{25 - x^2 - y^2}\)) and a flat floor (\(z = 0\)). For a closed surface, the outward flux through the whole skin equals the total divergence inside:

$$ \text{flux out} = \iiint_G \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\;dV $$

So instead of two separate surface integrals (dome and floor), we do one triple integral.

Step 2 — The divergence, term by term

\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = P_x + Q_y + R_z\): differentiate the \(\mathbf{i}\)-part by \(x\), the \(\mathbf{j}\)-part by \(y\), the \(\mathbf{k}\)-part by \(z\), and add.

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(8x^3) = 24x^2 $$
$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(8y^3) = 24y^2 $$
$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(8z^3) = 24z^2 $$
$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 24x^2 + 24y^2 + 24z^2 $$
$$ = 24\left(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\right) $$
Step 3 — Why spherical coordinates?

Two reasons point the same way: the solid is part of a ball, and the integrand contains \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\). In spherical coordinates \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \rho^2\) (\(\rho\) is the distance from the origin), so

$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 24\rho^2 $$
Step 4 — The limits (where each one comes from)

First, what is the solid? Square both sides of the dome's equation:

$$ z = \sqrt{25 - x^2 - y^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; z^2 = 25 - x^2 - y^2 $$
$$ \Longrightarrow\; x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 25 $$

That's a sphere of radius 5. The square root only gives \(z \ge 0\), so it's just the top half; the floor \(z = 0\) closes it off. The solid is the upper half-ball of radius 5.

Now each coordinate, one at a time:

  • \(\rho\) — how far from the origin? Every point in the solid lies between the centre (\(\rho = 0\)) and the dome. On the dome \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 25\), i.e. \(\rho^2 = 25\), so \(\rho = 5\). → \(0 \le \rho \le 5\)
  • \(\phi\) — how far down from the top? \(\phi\) is measured from the positive \(z\)-axis. Straight up is \(\phi = 0\). The floor \(z = 0\) is where \(\rho\cos\phi = 0\), i.e. \(\cos\phi = 0\), so \(\phi = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\). We stop there because nothing in the solid is below the floor. → \(0 \le \phi \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\)
  • \(\theta\) — which way round? Exactly as in polar coordinates. From above, the solid covers the whole disc \(r \le 5\) — every direction around the \(z\)-axis. → \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\)
$$ 0 \le \rho \le 5, \qquad 0 \le \phi \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi $$
⚠ The most common mistake: letting \(\phi\) go to \(\pi\). That's the whole ball (down to the bottom), and it doubles the answer. Half-ball above the \(xy\)-plane → \(\phi\) stops at \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
All three limits are plain numbers — no \(\rho\) in the \(\phi\) limits, nothing depending on anything else. That's what lets the integral split into three easy pieces in Step 6.
Step 5 — Put in the Jacobian

In spherical coordinates \(dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\). Multiply the divergence by it:

$$ \iiint_G 24\rho^2\,dV $$
$$ = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^5 24\rho^2\cdot\rho^2\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
$$ = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^5 24\rho^4\sin\phi\;d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta $$
Step 6 — Split it into three separate integrals

All the limits are plain numbers, and \(24\rho^4\sin\phi\) is (a \(\rho\)-part) × (a \(\phi\)-part) with no \(\theta\) at all. When that happens the triple integral is just three single integrals multiplied together:

$$ = 24 \cdot \left(\int_0^5 \rho^4\,d\rho\right) $$
$$ \times\left(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\phi\,d\phi\right)\cdot\left(\int_0^{2\pi} d\theta\right) $$
Step 7 — Do each one
$$ \int_0^5 \rho^4\,d\rho = \left[\frac{\rho^5}{5}\right]_0^5 = \frac{3125}{5} = 625 $$
$$ \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\phi\,d\phi = \Big[-\cos\phi\Big]_0^{\pi/2} $$
$$ = \left(-\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) - \left(-\cos 0\right) = 0 + 1 = 1 $$
$$ \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta = 2\pi $$
Step 8 — Multiply
$$ 24 \cdot 625 \cdot 1 \cdot 2\pi = 15000 \cdot 2\pi = 30000\pi $$
Outward flux \(= 30000\pi \approx 94{,}248\)
Don't forget the Jacobian \(\rho^2\sin\phi\): the integrand becomes \(24\rho^4\sin\phi\), not \(24\rho^2\).

Q5. A Triple Integral That Vanishes by Symmetry Coordinates

Tap to enlarge
Formula you need
$$ x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta $$
$$ dz\,dx\,dy = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
The idea The \(x, y\) limits describe a full disc centred at the origin. The integrand \(2x\) is positive on the right half and exactly as negative on the left half — so the total must be zero.
Step 1 — How to read the limits of a triple integral

Read the limits from the inside out. Each pair of limits belongs to the \(d\) that sits in the same position:

$$ \int_{\color{#1e8449}{-4}}^{\color{#1e8449}{4}}\int_{\color{#c0392b}{-\sqrt{16-y^2}}}^{\color{#c0392b}{\sqrt{16-y^2}}}\int_{\color{#2471a3}{0}}^{\color{#2471a3}{16-4x^2-4y^2}} 2x\;{\color{#2471a3}{dz}}\,{\color{#c0392b}{dx}}\,{\color{#1e8449}{dy}} $$
  • Inside (\(dz\)): for each point \((x, y)\), how low and how high \(z\) goes.
  • Middle (\(dx\)): for each \(y\), how far left and right \(x\) goes.
  • Outside (\(dy\)): the overall range of \(y\).

The outer two (\(x\) and \(y\)) describe the solid's shadow on the \(xy\)-plane. The inner one (\(z\)) describes its floor and roof. Let's decode each.

Step 2 — The \(x\) and \(y\) limits: what shape is the shadow?

The \(x\)-limits are \(\pm\sqrt{16 - y^2}\). Square \(x = \sqrt{16 - y^2}\):

$$ x^2 = 16 - y^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 + y^2 = 16 $$

That's a circle of radius 4. So for each \(y\), \(x\) goes from the circle's left edge (\(-\sqrt{16 - y^2}\)) to its right edge (\(+\sqrt{16 - y^2}\)) — a horizontal strip across the circle. Then \(y\) runs from \(-4\) (the bottom of the circle) to \(4\) (the top), stacking strips until the whole disc is covered.

A full disc of radius 4 centred at the origin is easy in polar/cylindrical coordinates:

$$ 0 \le r \le 4 \;\;(\text{centre out to the circle}) $$
$$ 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi \;\;(\text{all the way round}) $$
Step 3 — The \(z\) limits: floor and roof

The bottom limit \(z = 0\) is the floor (the \(xy\)-plane). The top limit is the roof. Notice it only depends on \(x^2 + y^2\), which is \(r^2\):

$$ z = 16 - 4x^2 - 4y^2 $$
$$ = 16 - 4(x^2 + y^2) = 16 - 4r^2 $$

That's an upside-down bowl with its top at \(z = 16\) (when \(r = 0\)). So for every point of the shadow, \(z\) goes from the floor up to the bowl:

$$ 0 \le z \le 16 - 4r^2 $$
Step 4 — Convert the integrand and \(dV\)
CartesianCylindricalwhy
\(2x\)\(2r\cos\theta\)\(x = r\cos\theta\)
\(dz\,dx\,dy\)\(r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\)the Jacobian \(r\)
\(z\): \(0 \to 16 - 4x^2 - 4y^2\)\(0 \to 16 - 4r^2\)\(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\)
\(x, y\): the disc\(r: 0 \to 4\), \(\theta: 0 \to 2\pi\)Step 2
Step 5 — The integral in cylindrical coordinates
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^4\!\!\int_0^{16 - 4r^2} 2r\cos\theta \cdot r\;dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
$$ = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^4\!\!\int_0^{16 - 4r^2} 2r^2\cos\theta\;dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
Step 6 — Inside first: the \(z\)-integral

Nothing in \(2r^2\cos\theta\) contains \(z\), so it's a constant here. Integrating a constant gives constant × (top − bottom):

$$ \int_0^{16 - 4r^2} 2r^2\cos\theta\;dz = 2r^2\cos\theta\,\big(16 - 4r^2 - 0\big) $$
Step 7 — Separate the \(\theta\) part from the \(r\) part

What's left is (something with only \(\theta\)) × (something with only \(r\)), and the limits are plain numbers, so it splits:

$$ \left(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta\right) $$
$$ \times \left(\int_0^4 2r^2\big(16 - 4r^2\big)\,dr\right) $$
Step 8 — The \(\theta\)-integral is 0, so everything is 0
$$ \int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta = \Big[\sin\theta\Big]_0^{2\pi} $$
$$ = \sin 2\pi - \sin 0 = 0 - 0 = 0 $$

Zero times anything is zero, so you don't even need the \(r\)-integral. (For the record it's \(\int_0^4 (32r^2 - 8r^4)\,dr = \tfrac{2048}{3} - \tfrac{8192}{5} = -\tfrac{14336}{15}\), but it's multiplied by 0.)

Why it had to be 0: the region is a perfect circle around the \(z\)-axis, and \(2x\) is positive on the right half (\(x > 0\)) and exactly as negative on the left half — that's the picture at the top. The halves cancel.
\(0\)
The upper limit \(16 - 4r^2\) is negative when \(r > 2\), so as written the integral partly runs "downwards". That doesn't change the answer — the \(\theta\)-integral is zero whatever the radial part is — but if the intended disc was \(r \le 2\) (where \(z = 16 - 4r^2\) meets \(z = 0\)), the answer is still 0.

Q6. Potential Function; Volume of a Hemisphere Unit 6

Tap to enlarge

(a)(i) Is it conservative? — the slide method

Formula you need
$$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{conservative} $$
$$ \int_A^B \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(B) - \phi(A) $$

Q7. Divergence Theorem; Surface Integral on a Plane Unit 7

Tap to enlarge

(a) Constant divergence → flux = 7 × volume

Formula you need
$$ \oiint_S \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_G \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F}\;dV $$
$$ \text{cylindrical: } dV = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
The idea The divergence works out to a constant, 7. That means "7 units produced per unit volume", so the outward flux is just 7 times the volume of the solid — and the volume is an easy cylindrical integral.
Step 1 — Why the divergence theorem?

The region is closed in: the bowl \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\) on top and the floor \(z = 0\) underneath. For a closed surface, the flux out through the whole skin equals the total divergence inside, so one triple integral replaces two surface integrals (bowl and floor).

Step 2 — The divergence, term by term

\(P = 2x\), \(Q = 2y\), \(R = 3z\). Differentiate each by its own letter and add:

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2x) = 2, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2y) = 2 $$
$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(3z) = 3 $$
$$ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{F} = 2 + 2 + 3 = 7 $$
Step 3 — A constant comes straight out
$$ \iiint_G 7\;dV = 7\iiint_G dV = 7 \times (\text{volume of } G) $$

So all we need is the volume of the solid under the bowl.

Step 4 — The limits (floor, roof, shadow)
  • Floor and roof (\(z\)): from the floor \(z = 0\) up to the bowl. In cylindrical coordinates \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\), so the roof is \(z = 4 - r^2\). → \(0 \le z \le 4 - r^2\)
  • Shadow (\(r\)): the bowl touches the floor where \(4 - r^2 = 0\), i.e. \(r = 2\). So the shadow is the disc of radius 2. → \(0 \le r \le 2\)
  • Around (\(\theta\)): the disc goes all the way round. → \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\)
Step 5 — Set up the volume integral
$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^{4 - r^2} r\;dz\,dr\,d\theta $$
Step 6 — Inside first: \(dz\)

\(r\) is a constant while integrating in \(z\), so it's \(r\) × (top − bottom):

$$ \int_0^{4 - r^2} r\;dz = r\big(4 - r^2\big) = 4r - r^3 $$
Step 7 — Next: \(dr\)
$$ \int_0^2 \big(4r - r^3\big)\,dr = \left[2r^2 - \frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 $$
$$ = \left(8 - \frac{16}{4}\right) - 0 = 8 - 4 = 4 $$
Step 8 — Last: \(d\theta\)
$$ V = \int_0^{2\pi} 4\;d\theta = 4 \cdot 2\pi = 8\pi $$
Step 9 — Multiply by the divergence
$$ \text{flux} = 7 \times 8\pi = 56\pi $$
Outward flux \(= 56\pi \approx 175.9\)

(b) \(\iint (x + y)\,dS\) on a plane

Formula you need
$$ \iint_\sigma f\,dS = \iint_R f\,\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\;dA $$
The idea A tilted plane is bigger than its shadow by a constant factor. Find that factor, then integrate \(x + y\) over the shadow triangle. (\(x + y\) contains no \(z\), so nothing needs substituting.)
Step 1 — Write the plane as \(z = g(x, y)\)

The formula needs the surface solved for \(z\). Move the \(x\) and \(y\) terms across:

$$ 2x + 3y + z = 6 \;\Longrightarrow\; z = 6 - 2x - 3y $$
Step 2 — The stretch factor \(\sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1}\)
$$ g_x = -2, \qquad g_y = -3 $$
$$ \sqrt{g_x^2 + g_y^2 + 1} = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + (-3)^2 + 1} $$
$$ = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 1} = \sqrt{14} $$

For a plane this is a constant: every bit of the tilted plane is \(\sqrt{14} \approx 3.74\) times bigger than its shadow. Constants come outside the integral.

Step 3 — The integrand \(f = x + y\)

\(f\) has no \(z\) in it, so nothing to substitute. (If it did — like \(yz\) in Q12 — you'd replace \(z\) with \(6 - 2x - 3y\) here.)

Step 4 — The shadow and its limits

"First octant" means \(x \ge 0\), \(y \ge 0\), \(z \ge 0\). The condition \(z \ge 0\) is

$$ 6 - 2x - 3y \ge 0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; 2x + 3y \le 6 $$

The edge \(2x + 3y = 6\) hits the \(x\)-axis at \(y = 0 \Rightarrow x = 3\), and the \(y\)-axis at \(x = 0 \Rightarrow y = 2\). Together with \(x, y \ge 0\), the shadow is the triangle with corners \((0, 0)\), \((3, 0)\), \((0, 2)\):

  • Outer (\(x\)): the triangle spans \(x = 0\) to \(x = 3\). → \(0 \le x \le 3\)
  • Inner (\(y\)): for each \(x\), \(y\) goes from the bottom edge \(y = 0\) up to the slanted edge. Solve \(2x + 3y = 6\) for \(y\): \(y = \tfrac{6 - 2x}{3}\). → \(0 \le y \le \tfrac{6 - 2x}{3}\)
Step 5 — Set up
$$ \iint_\sigma (x + y)\,dS $$
$$ = \sqrt{14}\int_0^3\!\!\int_0^{(6-2x)/3} (x + y)\,dy\,dx $$
Step 6 — Inside first: \(dy\) (treat \(x\) as a constant)
$$ \int_0^{(6-2x)/3} (x + y)\,dy = \left[xy + \frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^{(6-2x)/3} $$

Put in \(y = \tfrac{6 - 2x}{3}\) (the bottom limit 0 gives 0):

$$ x\cdot\frac{6 - 2x}{3} = 2x - \frac{2}{3}x^2 $$
$$ \frac12\left(\frac{6 - 2x}{3}\right)^2 = \frac{36 - 24x + 4x^2}{18} $$
$$ = 2 - \frac43 x + \frac29 x^2 $$

Add them, collecting the plain numbers, the \(x\)'s and the \(x^2\)'s:

$$ 2 + \left(2 - \tfrac43\right)x + \left(-\tfrac23 + \tfrac29\right)x^2 $$
$$ = 2 + \tfrac23 x - \tfrac49 x^2 $$
Step 7 — Then: \(dx\) from 0 to 3
$$ \int_0^3 \left(2 + \tfrac23 x - \tfrac49 x^2\right) dx = \left[2x + \frac{x^2}{3} - \frac{4x^3}{27}\right]_0^3 $$
$$ = 2(3) + \frac{9}{3} - \frac{4(27)}{27} = 6 + 3 - 4 = 5 $$
Step 8 — Multiply by the stretch factor
$$ \sqrt{14} \times 5 = 5\sqrt{14} $$
\(\iint_\sigma (x + y)\,dS = 5\sqrt{14} \approx 18.71\)
Check The triangle has area 3 and its centroid is \((1, \tfrac23)\), so the average of \(x + y\) is \(\tfrac53\) and \(\iint_R (x + y)\,dA = 3 \times \tfrac53 = 5\). ✓
